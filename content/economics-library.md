## Source 1.1 — *Introduction to Economics* (university module, September 2019)

**Whole-source summary.** A six-chapter teaching module that moves from what economics is (scarcity, choice, economic systems) through microeconomics (demand and supply, consumer behaviour, production and cost, market structures) to macroeconomics (national income, price indices, business cycles, unemployment, inflation, policy). Its running thread is that unlimited wants meet scarce resources, so every society must choose what, how, and for whom to produce. Examples and numbers use Ethiopia and the birr, so they would need adapting for a Jordan-focused site; Category 5 now supplies sourced Jordan and Ethiopia context (the module itself predates Ethiopia's July 2024 exchange-rate reform).

**Key points**

- Economics is the study of allocating scarce resources efficiently to satisfy unlimited wants.

- Microeconomics covers individual units (households, firms, markets); macroeconomics covers economy-wide aggregates.

- Price is set where demand meets supply; elasticity measures how responsive buyers and sellers are.

- Firms decide using production and cost curves, and market structure determines pricing power.

- Macro performance is tracked with GDP/GNP, price indices, unemployment, and inflation, and governments steer it with monetary and fiscal policy.

**Chapter map:** Ch. 1 Basics of Economics · Ch. 2 Demand and Supply · Ch. 3 Consumer Behaviour · Ch. 4 Production and Cost · Ch. 5 Market Structure · Ch. 6 Fundamental Concepts of Macroeconomics.

### Chapter 1 — Basics of Economics

#### 1.1.1 Definition of economics

**Summary:** Definition: Economics is the social science that studies how people, firms and governments allocate scarce resources to satisfy unlimited wants. Overview: The word comes from the Greek for managing a household, and the subject became distinct with Adam Smith's *Wealth of Nations* (1776). The field exists because wants outrun the means available to meet them, so every society needs some way of deciding what is produced and who gets it. Economists study those choices through four lenses the module names: wealth, welfare, scarcity and growth. The module also notes that no universally accepted definition exists. **Key highlights**

- Four definitional lenses: wealth, welfare, scarcity, growth.

- Called a "science of choice".

- Objective: maximum fulfilment of wants through efficient allocation.

#### 1.1.2 The rationale for studying economics

**Summary:** Definition: The rationale for studying economics is that human material wants are unlimited while economic resources are limited, which forces choices on individuals, families and nations. Overview: Because no one can have everything, every decision, from what to buy to how to tax, involves a trade-off. Economics supplies a framework for judging those trade-offs by efficiency (using resources well), equity (sharing outcomes fairly) and stability (avoiding sharp swings). It also looks for forms of economic organization that raise living standards or spread well-being more fairly. **Key highlights**

- Choice sits at the heart of all decision-making.

- Outcomes are judged by efficiency, equity, and stability.

- Economists also look for forms of economic organization that raise living standards or spread well-being more fairly.

#### 1.1.3 Microeconomics vs macroeconomics

**Summary:** Definition: Microeconomics analyzes individual decision-making units and how they interact in specific markets, while macroeconomics analyzes the aggregate behaviour of the whole economy. Overview: The split exists because questions differ in scale: why one price changes is a micro question, while why a country's output, prices or jobs move together is a macro one. Micro uses the demand and supply of particular goods to explain individual prices, outputs and incomes. Macro uses aggregate demand and supply to explain national income, the general price level and employment. The two are complementary, and macro cannot be studied in isolation from micro. **Key highlights**

- Micro: individual prices, outputs, and incomes; main tools are demand and supply of particular goods.

- Macro: national income, general price level, employment; main tools are aggregate demand and supply.

- Newer branches include development, environmental, and welfare economics.

#### 1.1.4 Positive vs normative analysis

**Summary:** Definition: Positive economics describes what was, is or will be and can be checked against facts, while normative economics says what ought to be and rests on value judgments. Overview: The distinction exists so that factual disagreements can be separated from disagreements about values. A statement such as inflation is 12 percent can be settled with data, but a statement such as the poor should pay no taxes depends on one's view of fairness. Keeping the two apart helps readers see when an economic claim is a finding and when it is a policy preference. **Key highlights**

- "Inflation is 12 percent" is positive; "the poor should pay no taxes" is normative.

- Positive disagreements are settled with evidence; normative ones are matters of opinion.

- Useful framing for any site section that mixes facts with policy opinion.

#### 1.1.5 Inductive vs deductive reasoning

**Summary:** Definition: Inductive reasoning builds general theories from specific observations, while deductive reasoning starts from assumptions or axioms and derives specific conclusions. Overview: A theory is a simplified picture of reality, and economists need systematic ways to build and test such pictures. The inductive route selects a problem, collects and analyzes data, and establishes cause and effect. The deductive route identifies a problem, states assumptions, forms hypotheses and tests them against facts. Both routes aim to produce theories of how economies behave. **Key highlights**

- Inductive steps: select a problem, collect and analyze data, establish cause and effect.

- Deductive steps: identify the problem, state assumptions, form hypotheses, test them.

#### 1.1.6 Scarcity and economic resources

**Summary:** Definition: Scarcity is the permanent imbalance between unlimited wants and the finite means of satisfying them. Overview: It exists because the four factors of production (labour, land, capital and entrepreneurship) are limited while wants keep growing. A free resource, such as sunshine, exists in more than the desired amount at zero price, whereas an economic resource is scarce and has to be rationed. Scarcity is universal and lasting, unlike a shortage, which is a short-term, specific problem at the going price. It is the reason societies must choose how to use their resources. **Key highlights**

- Four factors of production and their rewards: labour (wage), land (rent), capital (interest), entrepreneurship (profit).

- Entrepreneurs organize production, make policy decisions, innovate, and bear risk of loss.

- Scarcity does not mean shortage.

#### 1.1.7 Choice and opportunity cost

**Summary:** Definition: Opportunity cost is the value of the next best alternative sacrificed in order to get one more unit of something. Overview: Scarcity forces choice, and every choice gives up an alternative, so the chain runs from scarcity to limited output, to choice, to opportunity cost. It is measured in goods and services forgone rather than in money, which makes it usable even for options that have no price. When the opportunity cost of an activity rises, people tend to substitute away from it. **Key highlights**

- Chain: scarcity → limited output → choice → opportunity cost.

- Measured in goods and services forgone, not money.

- When an activity's opportunity cost rises, people substitute away from it.

#### 1.1.8 Production possibilities frontier (PPF) and economic growth

**Summary:** Definition: The production possibilities frontier (PPF) is a curve showing the combinations of goods a society can produce when it uses all its resources efficiently with given technology. Overview: It exists to show scarcity, choice and opportunity cost in a single picture. Points on the curve are efficient, points inside are attainable but waste resources, and points outside are unattainable. Moving along the curve means producing more of one good by giving up some of another, and the curve is concave because resources are not equally adaptable to every use (the law of increasing opportunity cost). Growth appears as an outward shift, caused by more or better resources or better technology. **Key highlights**

- Illustrates scarcity, choice, and opportunity cost in one picture.

- Law of increasing opportunity cost makes the PPF concave, because resources are not equally adaptable to every use.

- Growth comes from more or better resources, or better technology; gains in one sector alone cause asymmetric growth.

#### 1.1.9 The three basic economic questions

**Summary:** Definition: The three basic economic questions are what to produce, how to produce it, and for whom. Overview: Every economy must answer them because resources are scarce. What is the allocation of resources among uses, such as consumer versus capital goods, civil versus military, necessities versus luxuries. How is the choice of technique, labour-intensive or capital-intensive, which depends on factor supplies and relative prices. For whom is the distribution of the national product. Economic systems are best understood as different ways of answering these questions. **Key highlights**

- What: allocation of resources (consumer vs capital goods, civil vs military, necessities vs luxuries).

- How: choice of technique, labour-intensive or capital-intensive, depending on factor supplies and relative prices.

- For whom: distribution of the national product.

#### 1.1.10 Capitalist (market) economy

**Summary:** Definition: A capitalist (market) economy is a system in which the means of production are privately owned, activity is driven by private profit and government intervention is minimal. Overview: It is one answer to the basic economic questions, with the price mechanism answering them instead of a planner. Private property, consumer sovereignty, the profit motive, competition and self-interest guide what is produced and how. The module credits it with flexibility, entrepreneurship, higher per-capita income and capital formation, and faults it for income inequality, unbalanced development, labour exploitation and negative externalities such as pollution. **Key highlights**

- Features: private property, consumer sovereignty, profit motive, competition, self-interest.

- Strengths: flexibility, entrepreneurship, higher per-capita income, capital formation.

- Weaknesses: income inequality, unbalanced development, labour exploitation, negative externalities such as pollution.

#### 1.1.11 Command (socialist) economy

**Summary:** Definition: A command (socialist) economy is a system in which the state owns and controls production and distribution, and central planning allocates resources. Overview: It aims to direct resources toward social goals rather than private profit. Collective ownership and a strong government role are meant to give relative income equality, avoid wasteful competition and spread growth across regions. Because there is no automatic price mechanism, planners must make the decisions, and the module points to weak incentives, little economic freedom and red tape as the costs. **Key highlights**

- Features: collective ownership, central planning, strong government role, relative income equality.

- Strengths: no wasteful competition, balanced regional growth, fewer private monopolies.

- Weaknesses: no automatic price mechanism, weak incentives, little economic freedom, red tape.

#### 1.1.12 Mixed economy

**Summary:** Definition: A mixed economy is a system in which the public and private sectors coexist. Overview: It exists to combine the benefits of market and command systems. Strategic industries tend to be public while consumer and small-scale industry are private, the price mechanism works in the private sector, and the government regulates essential goods and prices. Governments can use progressive taxation, subsidies and limits on property holdings to promote equality. The risks are inefficiency in both sectors, economic fluctuations, and corruption and black markets if rules are weakly enforced. **Key highlights**

- Price mechanism works in the private sector; the government regulates essential goods and prices.

- Tools for equality: progressive taxation, subsidies, limits on property holdings.

- Risks: inefficiency in both sectors, economic fluctuations, corruption, and black markets if rules are weakly enforced.

#### 1.1.13 Decision-making units and the circular flow model

**Summary:** Definition: The circular flow model is a diagram of how households, firms and government exchange resources, goods and money in an economy. Overview: In a closed economy these three are the decision-makers, and they meet in the product market (goods and services) and the factor market (inputs). Households sell resources and buy goods, and firms buy resources and sell goods, so resources and goods move one way while money moves the other. In the two-sector version, household income equals firms' spending on factors and firm revenue equals household consumption. Government provides public goods and can influence households, firms and markets. **Key highlights**

- Households sell resources and buy goods; firms buy resources and sell goods.

- Two-sector model: household income equals firms' spending on factors; firm revenue equals household consumption.

- Government provides public goods and can influence households, firms, and markets.

### Chapter 2 — Theory of Demand and Supply

#### 1.1.14 Demand and the law of demand

**Summary:** Definition: Demand is desire for a good backed by purchasing power, meaning the buyer must be both willing and able to pay; the law of demand says that, other things equal, quantity demanded rises as price falls. Overview: The concept exists because wants only affect a market when buyers can afford them. Demand can be shown as a schedule, a downward-sloping curve or a function of price, and market demand is the horizontal sum of individual demands. The key assumption is *ceteris paribus* (other things equal), which lets the curve isolate the effect of price alone. **Key highlights**

- Shown as a schedule, a downward-sloping curve, or a function$Q_{d}\  = \ f(P)$.

- Market demand is the horizontal sum of individual demands.

- *Ceteris paribus* (other things equal) is the key assumption.

#### 1.1.15 Determinants of demand and shifts of the curve

**Summary:** Definition: The determinants of demand are the non-price factors (tastes, income, prices of related goods, expectations and the number of buyers) that shift the whole demand curve. Overview: A price change moves you along a given demand curve, but a change in any other determinant shifts the entire curve. For normal goods demand rises with income, and for inferior goods it falls. A change in the price of a substitute moves demand in the same direction, while a change in the price of a complement moves it the opposite way. The module also notes Giffen goods as an exception to the usual demand pattern. **Key highlights**

- Normal goods: demand rises with income. Inferior goods: demand falls with income.

- Substitutes: price of one moves demand for the other in the same direction. Complements: opposite direction.

- The summary also notes Giffen goods as an exception to the usual demand pattern.

#### 1.1.16 Elasticity of demand

**Summary:** Definition: Elasticity of demand is a unit-free measure of how strongly quantity demanded responds to a change in price (price elasticity), income (income elasticity) or another good's price (cross elasticity). Overview: It exists because the law of demand gives only the direction of a response, not its size. It is a ratio of percentage changes. Price elasticity above 1 is elastic (luxury-like), between 0 and 1 is inelastic (necessity-like), and equal to 1 is unitary. How elastic demand is depends on the availability of substitutes, whether the good is a necessity or a luxury, the share of income spent on it, and time, since demand is more elastic in the long run. **Key highlights**

- Price elasticity above 1 is elastic (luxury-like); between 0 and 1 is inelastic (necessity-like); equal to 1 is unitary.

- Drivers: availability of substitutes, necessity vs luxury, share of income spent, and time (more elastic in the long run).

- Cross elasticity is positive for substitutes, negative for complements, and zero for unrelated goods; negative income elasticity marks an inferior good.

#### 1.1.17 Supply and the law of supply

**Summary:** Definition: Supply is the quantity of a good producers are willing and able to offer at each price; the law of supply says that, other things equal, quantity supplied rises as price rises. Overview: Higher prices make production more rewarding, which is why sellers offer more. The curve shifts when its determinants change: input prices, technology, prices of related products, taxes and subsidies, expectations and the number of sellers (and weather, for agriculture). Cheaper inputs or better technology shift supply outward, and market supply is the horizontal sum of individual supplies. **Key highlights**

- Determinants: input prices, technology, prices of related products, taxes and subsidies, expectations, and number of sellers (weather matters for agriculture).

- Cheaper inputs or better technology shift supply outward.

- Market supply is the horizontal sum of individual supplies.

#### 1.1.18 Elasticity of supply

**Summary:** Definition: Elasticity of supply measures how responsive quantity supplied is to a change in price. Overview: It exists to show how easily producers can expand or cut output when prices move. Supply can be elastic (a small price change causes a large change in output), inelastic, unitary, perfectly elastic (horizontal) or perfectly inelastic (vertical). The module notes that the point method is the one most commonly used to measure it. **Key highlights**

- Elastic supply: a small price change causes a large change in output.

- The point method is the one most commonly used.

#### 1.1.19 Market equilibrium

**Summary:** Definition: Market equilibrium is the price and quantity at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. Overview: It matters because it is where the market clears. Above the equilibrium price there is a surplus, which pushes the price down, and below it there is a shortage, which pushes the price up. Higher demand raises both price and quantity, while higher supply lowers price and raises quantity. When demand and supply both increase, quantity clearly rises but the effect on price depends on which shift is larger. The module works through numerical equilibrium examples. **Key highlights**

- Higher demand raises both price and quantity; higher supply lowers price and raises quantity.

- When demand and supply both change, the effect on quantity is clear but the effect on price depends on which shift is larger.

- The module works through numerical equilibrium examples.

### Chapter 3 — Theory of Consumer Behaviour

#### 1.1.20 Consumer preferences and utility

**Summary:** Definition: Utility is the satisfaction a good provides, or its power to satisfy wants, and consumer preferences describe how a consumer ranks bundles of goods. Overview: Consumer theory needs a way to describe choice, so it assumes consumers compare bundles and judge one strictly better, weakly better or equally good (indifferent). Utility is subjective, consumer-centred and differs from person to person. Two ways of comparing it are developed in the next concepts: cardinal (measure it in units) and ordinal (only rank it). **Key highlights**

- Preference relations: strict preference, weak preference, indifference.

- Utility differs from person to person.

- Two ways to compare utility: cardinal and ordinal.

#### 1.1.21 Cardinal utility theory and diminishing marginal utility

**Summary:** Definition: Cardinal utility theory holds that satisfaction can be measured in units, and the law of diminishing marginal utility says that each additional unit adds less satisfaction than the one before. Overview: Total utility is overall satisfaction and marginal utility is the extra satisfaction from one more unit. The theory exists to explain why people buy less of a good as its price rises: each extra unit is worth less to them. A consumer with one good is in equilibrium where marginal utility equals price, and with several goods where marginal utility per unit of price is equal across goods. It assumes constant marginal utility of money and additive utility, and its limitations are that utility is hard to quantify and the marginal utility of money is not really constant. **Key highlights**

- A consumer with one good is in equilibrium where marginal utility equals price; with several goods, where MU/price is equal across goods.

- Assumes constant marginal utility of money and additive utility.

- Limitations: utility is hard to quantify, and the marginal utility of money is not really constant.

#### 1.1.22 Ordinal utility and indifference curves

**Summary:** Definition: Ordinal utility theory holds that satisfaction cannot be measured but can be ranked, and an indifference curve shows all combinations of two goods that give the same satisfaction. Overview: It was developed to avoid the measurement problem of the cardinal approach. A set of indifference curves forms an indifference map, with higher curves preferred. The curves slope downward, are convex to the origin because of a diminishing marginal rate of substitution, and do not intersect, assuming consistent (transitive) preferences. **Key highlights**

- Properties: downward-sloping, convex to the origin, higher curves preferred, curves do not intersect.

- Convexity reflects a diminishing marginal rate of substitution.

- Assumes consistent (transitive) preferences.

#### 1.1.23 Budget line and consumer equilibrium

**Summary:** Definition: The budget line shows every combination of two goods a consumer can afford given prices and income, and consumer equilibrium is the affordable bundle that gives the most satisfaction. Overview: It combines what the consumer wants (indifference curves) with what the consumer can afford (the budget line). The consumer maximizes utility where the budget line is tangent to the highest attainable indifference curve. A change in income shifts the line, and a change in a price changes its slope. The ordinal approach avoids the measurement problem of the cardinal approach. **Key highlights**

- Combines what the consumer wants (indifference curves) with what they can afford (budget line).

- The ordinal approach avoids the measurement problem of the cardinal approach.

### Chapter 4 — Theory of Production and Cost

#### 1.1.24 Production and the production function

**Summary:** Definition: Production is the transformation of inputs into outputs that have exchange value, and the production function is the technical relationship between inputs and the maximum output possible with existing technology. Overview: It exists to describe what a firm can make from what it uses. The inputs are land, labour, capital and entrepreneurial ability. Fixed inputs cannot be changed quickly while variable inputs can, and the short run is the period in which at least one input is fixed, so it is defined by the fixed input and not by a set number of months. A typical short-run setup holds capital fixed and varies labour. **Key highlights**

- Inputs: land, labour, capital, entrepreneurial ability.

- Typical short-run setup: capital fixed, labour variable.

- "Short run" is defined by the fixed input, not by a set number of months.

#### 1.1.25 Total, average, and marginal product; the law of variable proportions

**Summary:** Definition: Total product is overall output, average product is output per unit of the variable input and marginal product is the extra output from one more unit; the law of variable proportions (diminishing returns) says that beyond some point each added unit of the variable input adds less output. Overview: These measures show how output responds as a firm adds a variable input to a fixed one. Marginal product first rises, peaks and then falls, and it measures the slope of total product. The law assumes technology is fixed, and it explains the shape of the cost curves that follow. **Key highlights**

- Marginal product first rises, peaks, then falls; it measures the slope of total product.

- The law assumes technology is fixed.

#### 1.1.26 Stages of short-run production

**Summary:** Definition: The stages of short-run production are three regions of the production process, defined by how total, average and marginal product behave as more of the variable input is added. Overview: They exist to show where a firm should operate. In Stage I average product is rising and the fixed input is under-used, so it is not efficient. In Stage II marginal product is positive but declining, and this is the efficient region. In Stage III further input additions reduce total output. **Key highlights**

- Stage I: average product rising, fixed input under-used, not efficient.

- Stage II: diminishing marginal returns, the efficient region.

- Stage III: further input additions reduce total output.

#### 1.1.27 Types of cost: explicit, implicit, accounting, economic

**Summary:** Definition: Explicit costs are out-of-pocket payments for purchased inputs, implicit costs are the estimated value of self-owned inputs, accounting cost counts only explicit costs and economic cost counts both. Overview: The distinction exists because a firm's true cost includes what its owner's own resources could have earned elsewhere, not just what is paid out. So accounting profit equals revenue minus explicit cost, while economic profit equals revenue minus explicit and implicit cost. Whenever implicit costs are present, accounting profit exceeds economic profit by that amount. **Key highlights**

- Accounting profit = revenue − explicit cost; economic profit = revenue − (explicit + implicit cost).

- With implicit costs present, accounting profit exceeds economic profit by that amount.

#### 1.1.28 Short-run cost curves

**Summary:** Definition: Short-run cost curves show how a firm's costs change with output, splitting total cost into fixed cost (unavoidable at any output) and variable cost (which rises with output). Overview: From these come the per-unit curves: average fixed, average variable, average total and marginal cost. Average variable and average total cost are U-shaped because of the law of variable proportions, marginal cost mirrors marginal product, and average variable cost mirrors average product. A firm can avoid fixed costs only by shutting down. **Key highlights**

- Average variable and average total cost are U-shaped because of the law of variable proportions.

- Marginal cost mirrors marginal product, and average variable cost mirrors average product.

- A firm can avoid fixed costs only by shutting down.

### Chapter 5 — Market Structure

#### 1.1.29 Markets in physical and digital space

**Summary:** Definition: A market is any place or digital space where goods, services and ideas are exchanged. Overview: Markets exist to bring buyers and sellers together. The module contrasts physical markets, where buyers and sellers meet in person, with digital channels that carry product value from producer to consumer over networks. Digital marketing uses the internet, mobile phones, display advertising and other digital media. **Key highlights**

- Digital marketing uses the internet, mobile phones, display advertising, and other digital media.

- Useful bridge to the digital and gig economy concepts in Sources 1.2 and 3.1.

#### 1.1.30 Perfectly competitive market

**Summary:** Definition: Perfect competition is a market with so many buyers and sellers that none can influence price, so every firm is a price taker. Overview: It serves as the benchmark against which other market structures are compared. It rests on five assumptions: many buyers and sellers, a homogeneous product, perfect factor mobility, free entry and exit, and perfect market knowledge. The firm maximizes profit where marginal revenue equals marginal cost, and in this market price equals marginal revenue. In the short run a firm can earn economic profit, normal (zero) profit or a loss; the shutdown point is where price equals minimum average variable cost. **Key highlights**

- Five assumptions: many buyers and sellers, homogeneous product, perfect factor mobility, free entry and exit, perfect market knowledge.

- In the short run a firm can earn economic profit, normal (zero) profit, or a loss.

- Shutdown point: price equals minimum average variable cost; the industry is in equilibrium when the market clears.

#### 1.1.31 Monopoly

**Summary:** Definition: A monopoly is a market with a single seller, no close substitutes and blocked entry. Overview: Because the monopolist faces the whole downward-sloping market demand, it is a price maker rather than a price taker. Its power comes from barriers to entry, which the module groups into four sources: legal restriction (for example public utilities), control of key raw materials, efficiency and economies of scale (a natural monopoly), and patent rights. **Key highlights**

- Barriers to entry are the source of monopoly power.

- Four sources: legal restriction (e.g., public utilities), control of key raw materials, efficiency and economies of scale (natural monopoly), and patent rights.

#### 1.1.32 Monopolistic competition

**Summary:** Definition: Monopolistic competition is a market structure in which many firms sell differentiated products and entry is easy. Overview: It blends competition and monopoly. The competitive elements are the many firms and the absence of entry barriers; the monopoly element is differentiation (style, brand, quality), which gives each seller limited power over its loyal customers and some control over price within narrow limits. The module separates real from fancied (perceived) differentiation. **Key highlights**

- Competitive elements: many firms, no entry barriers.

- Monopoly element: differentiated products, so some control over price within narrow limits.

- The module separates real from fancied (perceived) differentiation.

#### 1.1.33 Oligopoly and comparing market structures

**Summary:** Definition: An oligopoly is a market dominated by a few large firms, each affected by its rivals' price and output decisions. Overview: Interdependence is the defining feature, and entry barriers are considerable. Because firms fear price wars, they lean on non-price competition such as advertising, warranties and after-sales service. Products may be homogeneous or differentiated, and a two-firm market is a duopoly. The table below compares all four market structures. **Key highlights**

- Defining feature is interdependence; entry barriers are considerable; products may be homogeneous or differentiated; a two-firm market is a duopoly.

- Comparison from the module:

### Chapter 6 — Fundamental Concepts of Macroeconomics

#### 1.1.34 Goals of macroeconomics

**Summary:** Definition: The goals of macroeconomics are the whole-economy outcomes policy tries to achieve: full employment, stable prices and sustainable growth. Overview: Macroeconomics studies the economy's aggregate behaviour, covering growth, employment, inflation, income distribution, macro policy and international trade, so performance is judged at the level of the whole economy. Alongside the three main goals, policy also manages budget and balance-of-payments deficits. The ultimate policy objective is sustainable growth and development. **Key highlights**

- Performance is judged at the whole-economy level.

Assets held to meet payment demands or for trading are increasingly marked to market, meaning valued by what the asset could be exchanged for between informed, willing parties. Loan novations, assignments, swaps and securitizations can create losses, which are recognized currently if done at arm's length, and stock lending and repurchase agreements (REPOs) are taxed by their substance, not their legal form. **Key highlights**

- Mark to market: value by what the asset could be exchanged for between informed, willing parties.

- Stock lending and repurchase agreements (REPOs) are taxed by their substance, not their legal form.

#### 4.1.4 Fees and other income

**Summary:** Definition: Fee income is what a bank earns for services rather than from lending, such as retail services, custody and advice, capital-market work, guarantees and debt factoring. Overview: Fees are recognized when earned and receivable, and fees for multi-year facilities may be spread over time. The rule is the same for commercial accounting and for tax. **Key highlights**

- Same rule for commercial accounting and tax.

#### 4.1.5 Interest payable

**Summary:** Definition: Interest payable is the interest a bank pays on its deposits and borrowings. Overview: It is normally deductible on an accrual basis. Exceptions apply when the interest looks like a dividend: above-market rates, payments tied to profits or limited-recourse loans. Interest on core regulatory capital (Tier 1, 2, 3) is not deductible in principle, and banks have used offshore vehicles to create capital that is deductible, which links to thin capitalization. Disallowed interest may be taxed like a dividend. **Key highlights**

- Interest on core regulatory capital (Tier 1, 2, 3) is not deductible in principle.

- Banks have used offshore vehicles to create capital that is deductible; this links to thin capitalization.

- Disallowed interest may be taxed like a dividend.

#### 4.1.6 Bad debts and loan-loss provisions

**Summary:** Definition: Bad debts and loan-loss provisions are amounts set aside, or written off, for loans that are unlikely to be repaid. Overview: Relief for impaired debts is especially important for banks because lending is their core business. Specific provisions (for identified problem loans) are usually relieved, with clawback if the debt is repaid or the provision proves excessive, while general provisions (against possible future problems) get less relief. Three approaches are described: commercial accounts (follow accounting rules, often specific provisions only), regulatory (tie relief to the minimum provisions the regulator requires) and charge-off (relief only after write-off, considered too restrictive). Unpaid interest on impaired loans goes to a suspense account and is taxed if later received. **Key highlights**

- Commercial accounts approach: follows accounting rules, often specific provisions only.

- Regulatory approach: ties relief to the minimum provisions the regulator requires, often disallowing general provisions.

- Charge-off approach: relief only after debts are written off as irrecoverable; considered too restrictive.

- Unpaid interest on impaired loans goes to a suspense account and is taxed if later received.

- Recapitalization: if public funds cover a bank's losses, the matching tax losses are written off too (no "double dipping").

- Table 1.1 examples (c. 2002): general provisions deductible in China (1% domestic, 3% foreign-invested enterprises), Singapore, Japan, Germany, and Italy with limits; not deductible in the UK, Netherlands, Hong Kong, Australia, Korea, New Zealand, or the US.

#### 4.1.7 Derivatives and other financial instruments

**Summary:** Definition: Derivatives and other financial instruments are contracts and securities that banks hold for client service, trading, hedging, speculation and as intermediaries. Overview: The usual practice is to mark them to market. The main exception is instruments held to hedge the bank's own risks, which may use accrual treatment if the hedge is documented and the other side is not marked to market. The consistency principle requires both sides of a hedge to be valued the same way, and detailed treatment is left to another chapter of the volume. **Key highlights**

- Consistency principle: both sides of a hedge valued the same way.

- Detailed treatment is left to another chapter of the volume.

#### 4.1.8 Foreign exchange gains and losses

**Summary:** Definition: Foreign exchange gains and losses are the changes in the domestic-currency value of a bank's foreign-currency assets and liabilities. Overview: Companies generally keep accounts in domestic currency. Gains and losses on monetary items, which are most of a bank's assets and liabilities, are recognized at the balance-sheet date, while fixed and intangible assets are converted at purchase and recognized on disposal. Hedged fixed assets may offset gains and losses so that only the net is taxed, and, rarely, banks working mainly in US dollars may keep tax accounts in foreign currency. **Key highlights**

- Hedged fixed assets may offset gains and losses so only the net is taxed.

- Rarely, banks working mainly in US dollars may keep tax accounts in foreign currency.

#### 4.1.9 Double tax relief

**Summary:** Definition: Double tax relief is a credit or deduction that stops the same income being taxed in two countries. Overview: Banks generally get the same foreign tax credit as other businesses, limited to the tax that would apply domestically, with any excess deductible as a cost. Lending abroad creates a special problem because foreign withholding tax hits gross interest while the bank's profit is only the margin. Some countries cap credit relief on interest (for example at 15%), anti-avoidance rules target routing through connected businesses, and tax sparing recognizes foreign tax that the foreign country's incentives spared. **Key highlights**

- Some countries cap credit relief on interest (e.g., 15%).

- Anti-avoidance rules target routing through connected businesses.

- "Tax sparing" recognizes foreign tax that was spared by the foreign country's incentives.

#### 4.1.10 Foreign banks, branches, and transfer pricing

**Summary:** Definition: Transfer pricing is the setting of prices for transactions between connected parties so that they reflect arm's-length prices, and this section also covers how foreign banks' branches and subsidiaries are taxed. Overview: Double tax agreements and OECD guidelines govern branches and subsidiaries. Interest a branch pays its foreign head office is not normally deductible, except on loans made in ordinary banking business. The OECD splits lending into six components, of which the first four (sourcing, assessing, negotiating, deciding) matter most in assigning profit, while investment banking profits are hard to locate, so a profit-splitting formula may be used. Modern finance moves information and assets across borders at the click of a button, which makes enforcement harder. **Key highlights**

- OECD splits lending into six components; the first four (sourcing, assessing, negotiating, deciding) matter most in assigning profit.

- Investment banking profits are hard to locate, so a profit-splitting formula may be used.

- Modern finance moves information and assets across borders at the click of a button, which makes enforcement harder.

#### 4.1.11 Turnover taxes on financial institutions

**Summary:** Definition: A turnover tax on financial institutions is a tax on a bank's gross interest and other income rather than on its net profit. Overview: Some countries use them because they raise revenue without needing to compute net profit. But they ignore ability to pay and hit thin-margin businesses hard, so they are not seen as best practice and are absent from most modern tax systems. **Key highlights**

- Not seen as best practice and absent from most modern tax systems.

#### 4.2.1 Stock market

**Summary:** Definition: The stock market is the network of exchanges and over-the-counter venues where investors buy and sell shares in publicly traded companies. Overview: It is broader than any single exchange: the NYSE and Nasdaq are components of a global system that also includes brokerages and OTC markets. Companies use it to raise capital by issuing shares, while investors use it to buy ownership stakes, receive possible dividends, vote on corporate matters, or sell shares later. The article notes that stock market is often used loosely to mean a particular exchange, but the term covers the whole trading system. **Key highlights**

- It connects companies seeking capital with investors seeking ownership opportunities.

- It includes exchanges, brokers and over-the-counter trading venues.

- It supports capital formation, liquidity, price discovery and corporate transparency.

#### 4.2.2 Public company

**Summary:** Definition: A public company is a company whose shares can be offered to the general public and traded through public markets. Overview: In the U.S. context described by the source, a company must register with the SEC before selling shares publicly and must comply with disclosure and financial-reporting rules. Public status gives a company access to a broad pool of investors, but it also brings reporting and regulatory obligations. The article identifies the IPO (initial public offering, a company's first public sale of shares) as the traditional route to going public and notes SPACs as an alternative route that emerged in the 2020s. **Key highlights**

#### 4.2.3 Share

**Summary:** Definition: A share, or stock, is a unit of ownership in a company. Overview: The proportion of a company owned by a shareholder depends on the number of shares issued and the number of shares that person holds. Large public companies may have millions or billions of shares, so one share can represent a very small ownership fraction. Owners may receive part of company profits as dividends and may have voting rights, depending on the share class. **Key highlights**

- A share represents an ownership claim, not a loan to the company.

- Ownership percentage depends on shares held relative to total shares issued.

- Common shares commonly provide voting rights; dividend rights depend on the company and share type.

#### 4.2.4 Primary market

**Summary:** Definition: The primary market is where a company first sells newly issued shares to investors. Overview: Buyers in this market purchase shares directly from the issuing company or through intermediaries involved in the offering. The source includes early investors, company insiders and financial underwriters among primary-market participants. It also includes private placements, in which a company sells shares directly to investors without a public registration process. **Key highlights**

- The company receives the proceeds from newly issued shares.

- IPOs occur in the primary market.

- Private placements are also part of the primary market.

#### 4.2.5 Secondary market

**Summary:** Definition: The secondary market is where investors trade existing shares with one another. Overview: After shares have been issued, subsequent purchases and sales generally occur in the secondary market. The issuing company does not receive the money from these later trades; the seller receives payment from the buyer. Exchanges and OTC venues can both host secondary-market trading. **Key highlights**

- Secondary-market trading transfers ownership between investors.

- The company does not receive proceeds from ordinary secondary-market share sales.

- Liquidity depends on the availability of willing buyers and sellers.

#### 4.2.6 Stock exchange

**Summary:** Definition: A stock exchange is an organized, regulated venue where securities are bought and sold. Overview: Exchanges are mostly electronic rather than physical places, but they provide a central system for trading and price information. They set listing and trading rules, publish real-time price information and help create liquidity by bringing together many buyers and sellers. Examples named in the article include the NYSE, Nasdaq, London Stock Exchange, Tokyo Stock Exchange and Shanghai Stock Exchange. **Key highlights**

- Exchanges provide transparency through price and trading information.

- Liquidity is easier when many participants trade the same security.

- Exchanges may cross-list shares from other markets.

#### 4.2.7 Over-the-counter market

**Summary:** Definition: An over-the-counter (OTC) market is a decentralized venue where securities are traded directly through brokers and dealers rather than on a formal exchange. Overview: OTC trading commonly involves smaller or less liquid companies that may not meet exchange-listing requirements. Because public information and trading scrutiny can be lower, investors may find it harder to obtain reliable information. The article describes OTC trading as occurring through broker and dealer networks using computer systems and telephone communication. **Key highlights**

- OTC trading is negotiated through brokers and dealers.

- It may involve less liquid securities than exchange-listed shares.

#### 5.1.1 Jordan's exchange-rate regime: the dinar peg

**Summary:** Definition: A fixed (pegged) exchange rate is a regime in which the central bank keeps its currency at a set value against another currency; Jordan holds the dinar at about 0.709 dinars per US dollar. Overview: The CBJ has kept this fixed dollar rate since 1995, and news reports at the time said it ended speculation about devaluation after the late-1980s debt crisis. The CBJ governor has called the peg the best and most appropriate policy for the economy. The trade-off, raised by commentators, is that Jordan must keep its monetary policy aligned with US conditions even when local conditions differ. **Key highlights**

- Fixed rate in place since 1995; a report on the CBJ's 2022 annual report describes an average of 709 fils per dollar (Jordan News).

- In November 1995 the CBJ told banks and money-changers that all dinar-dollar transactions should stay within 708 to 710 fils; the same package made the dinar convertible for current spending (UPI, 1995).

- Reserves were $27.2 billion at the end of May 2026, enough for 9.5 months of imports of goods and services (CBJ statement, Jordan Times); the IMF cites about $27 billion at the end of Q1 2026.

- Dollarisation of deposits fell to 18.1% by February 2026 from 18.8% a year earlier, which the CBJ reads as rising confidence in the dinar (Jordan Times).

#### 5.2.1 The birr and Ethiopia's 2024 move to a market-based exchange rate

**Summary:** Definition: A market-based (floating) exchange rate is one set by the supply and demand for foreign currency in the market, rather than fixed or managed by the central bank; the birr (ETB) is Ethiopia's currency. Overview: On 29 July 2024 the NBE issued Directive FXD/01/2024, ending a long-managed regime (a crawling peg, in the NBE's description) and allowing banks to buy and sell foreign currency at freely negotiated rates. - Before the reform the official rate was about 58.6 birr per dollar while the parallel-market rate was above 115 (East African Review).

- Other changes in the directive included letting residents open foreign-currency accounts and removing interest-rate ceilings (East African Review).

- Commentators at the time warned that a float could bring volatility and inflation, while supporters expected it to restore stability and private activity (Addis Standard op-ed; AllAfrica).

## Source 6.1 — Corporate Finance Institute: *Income Statement*

*Corporate Finance Institute (CFI), 24 November 2022, https://corporatefinanceinstitute.com/resources/accounting/income-statement/, accessed 22 November 2026.*

**Status:** Read in full (by the research assistant).

**Whole-source summary.** CFI explains that the income statement reports a company's revenues, expenses and profit or loss over a defined period. It presents a typical sequence from revenue through cost of goods sold, gross profit, operating expenses, operating income, interest, taxes and net income. The article also explains earnings per share, depreciation and amortization, EBITDA, and the use of income-statement drivers in financial models. It emphasizes that the statement is prepared using accrual-based accounting rather than simply recording cash received and paid.

**Key points**

- The income statement measures profitability over a period, such as a month, quarter or year.

- Revenue minus cost of goods sold equals gross profit.

- Operating expenses are deducted from gross profit to obtain operating income.

- Interest, other items and income taxes are then considered before net income.

- Net income divided by weighted average shares outstanding gives earnings per share.

- Depreciation and amortization are non-cash expenses that spread the cost of long-lived assets over time.

#### 6.1.1 Income statement

**Summary:** Definition: The income statement reports a company's revenues, expenses and resulting profit or loss for a period. Overview: It is also called a profit and loss statement, or P&L, although CFI notes that the terms are often used interchangeably. It covers a time period rather than a single date and helps users assess whether operations generated a profit. It is one of the three core financial statements and is commonly the starting point for financial models. **Key highlights**

- It covers a period, not a point in time.

- It shows profitability from operating and non-operating activities.

- It is also called a P&L.

#### 6.1.2 Revenue

**Summary:** Definition: Revenue is the income a company earns from selling goods or providing services. Overview: Revenue appears at the top of the income statement and is sometimes called sales or the top line. A company may have several revenue streams, such as product sales and service fees, which combine into total revenue. Revenue is recorded before deducting the costs of producing or delivering the related goods and services. **Key highlights**

- Revenue is the starting point of the income statement.

- It may come from multiple products or services.

- It is measured before direct costs are deducted.

#### 6.1.3 Cost of goods sold

**Summary:** Definition: Cost of goods sold, or COGS, includes the direct costs of producing goods sold or delivering services. Overview: CFI says the line may also be called cost of sales, especially for service businesses. Direct costs can include labor, parts, materials and an allocated share of expenses such as depreciation. COGS is subtracted from revenue to calculate gross profit. **Key highlights**

- COGS includes direct production or service-delivery costs.

- It may be labelled cost of sales.

- It is deducted from revenue to obtain gross profit.

#### 6.1.4 Gross profit

**Summary:** Definition: Gross profit is revenue minus cost of goods sold. Overview: It shows the profit remaining after direct costs but before operating expenses, interest and taxes. CFI identifies gross profit as a key subtotal and notes that analysts often compare it with total sales to calculate gross profit margin. A higher gross profit does not by itself mean high overall profitability, because operating and financing costs still need to be paid. **Key highlights**

- Gross profit = Revenue - COGS.

- It excludes operating expenses, interest and taxes.

- Gross margin compares gross profit with revenue.

#### 6.1.5 Operating expenses

**Summary:** Definition: Operating expenses are the indirect costs of running the business. Overview: CFI groups selling, general and administrative expenses under this broad category. Examples include salaries and wages, rent, office expenses, insurance, travel, and sometimes depreciation and amortization (non-cash charges that spread the cost of long-lived assets over time). These expenses are deducted from gross profit to arrive at operating income. **Key highlights**

- Operating expenses support the business but are not direct production costs.

- SG&A is a common label for selling, general and administrative expenses.

- They are deducted before operating income is calculated.

#### 6.1.6 Operating income

**Summary:** Definition: Operating income is profit from regular business operations before interest and taxes. Overview: CFI also calls it EBIT, meaning earnings before interest and taxes. It is calculated after revenue, COGS and operating expenses, but before non-operating income or expense, interest and taxes. It helps users see whether the core business is profitable independently of financing costs and tax effects. **Key highlights**

- Operating income = Gross profit - Operating expenses.

- It is also called EBIT.

- It excludes interest and income taxes.

#### 6.1.7 Interest expense

**Summary:** Definition: Interest expense is the cost a company pays for borrowed money. Overview: CFI says companies commonly show interest expense and interest income separately to reconcile the difference between EBIT and earnings before tax. Interest expense is linked to a company's debt schedule. It is deducted after operating income in the income statement. **Key highlights**

- Interest expense reflects the cost of debt.

- It is separate from operating income.

- It reduces pre-tax income.

#### 6.1.8 Income taxes

**Summary:** Definition: Income taxes are taxes charged on a company's pre-tax income. Overview: CFI states that income taxes are deducted from pre-tax income to arrive at net income. The tax expense can include both current taxes and future taxes. Tax rules and rates differ by jurisdiction, so the same pre-tax profit can produce different tax expenses in different countries. **Key highlights**

- Income taxes are deducted after pre-tax income.

- Tax expense may include current and deferred components.

- Tax treatment varies by jurisdiction.

#### 6.1.9 Net income

**Summary:** Definition: Net income is a company's profit after all expenses, interest and taxes. Overview: It is the bottom line of the income statement and can be a net profit or net loss. CFI explains that net income flows into retained earnings on the balance sheet, after any dividends are considered. It is also the usual starting point for the indirect method of preparing operating cash flow. **Key highlights**

- Net income = Pre-tax income - Income taxes.

- It is the income statement's bottom line.

- It links to retained earnings and operating cash flow.

#### 6.1.10 Earnings per share

**Summary:** Definition: Earnings per share, or EPS, is net income divided by weighted average shares outstanding. Overview: EPS expresses profit on a per-share basis, making it easier to compare results across periods or companies with different numbers of shares. It is a reported measure, not a measure of cash available to the company. It connects the income statement to the stock market concepts in Source 4.2. **Key highlights**

- EPS = Net income / Weighted average shares outstanding.

- It standardizes profit per share.

- It does not itself show cash flow.

#### 6.1.11 Single-step and multi-step formats

**Summary:** Definition: A single-step income statement subtracts total expenses from total revenues in one calculation, while a multi-step format presents subtotals such as gross profit and operating income. Overview: CFI's explanation follows a multi-step structure, separating COGS, gross profit, operating expenses, operating income, interest, taxes and net income. A single-step format is simpler because it groups revenues and expenses before calculating net income. The choice of format affects presentation, not the underlying economic events. **Key highlights**

- Multi-step statements show intermediate profit subtotals.

- Single-step statements use a simpler revenue-minus-expense structure.

- Both formats aim to arrive at net income.

#### 6.1.12 Accrual accounting

**Summary:** Definition: Accrual accounting records revenues and expenses when they are earned or incurred, rather than only when cash moves. Overview: CFI says the income statement uses accounting principles including matching and accruals, rather than presenting figures on a cash basis. This means a sale made on credit can increase revenue and accounts receivable before cash is received. It also means expenses can be recognized before the related cash is paid. **Key highlights**

- Revenue may be recognized before cash is received.

- Expenses may be recognized before cash is paid.

- This is why net income can differ from operating cash flow.

## Source 6.2 — Corporate Finance Institute: *Balance Sheet*

*Corporate Finance Institute (CFI), 24 November 2022, https://corporatefinanceinstitute.com/resources/accounting/balance-sheet/, accessed 22 November 2026.*

**Status:** Read in full (by the research assistant).

**Whole-source summary.** CFI explains that the balance sheet, also called the statement of financial position, shows what a company owns, owes and has left for owners at a specific date. It organizes assets, liabilities and shareholders' equity around the accounting equation. The article explains current and non-current classification, liquidity ordering, major asset and liability line items, retained earnings and common ratios. It also links balance-sheet changes to cash-flow preparation, including capital expenditure and depreciation.

**Key points**

- The balance sheet is a snapshot at a particular date.

- Assets equal liabilities plus shareholders' equity.

- Current items are expected to convert to cash or become due within one year; non-current items extend beyond one year.

- Assets are commonly ordered from most liquid to least liquid.

- Retained earnings accumulate net income kept in the business after dividends.

- The balance sheet supports liquidity, leverage, efficiency and return ratios.

#### 6.2.1 Balance sheet

**Summary:** Definition: The balance sheet reports a company's assets, liabilities and equity at a specific point in time. Overview: It is also called the statement of financial position. It provides a snapshot rather than a period measure, showing the resources a company controls, the claims of creditors and the residual interest of owners. It is used to assess financial strength, liquidity and capital structure. **Key highlights**

- It is a point-in-time statement.

- It has three main sections: assets, liabilities and equity.

- It is also called the statement of financial position.

#### 6.2.2 Accounting equation

**Summary:** Definition: The accounting equation states that assets equal liabilities plus equity. Overview: CFI presents this as the structure underlying the balance sheet. Assets are resources the business owns or controls; liabilities are obligations owed to others; equity is the residual interest remaining for owners after liabilities are deducted. The equation must hold for the balance sheet to balance. **Key highlights**

- Assets = Liabilities + Equity.

- Equity can also be viewed as assets minus liabilities.

- A change on one side must be matched elsewhere for the equation to hold.

#### 6.2.3 Assets

**Summary:** Definition: Assets are resources a company owns or controls that are expected to provide future economic benefit. Overview: CFI lists cash, inventory, property and equipment as examples. Assets are divided into current and non-current categories. Their balance-sheet value may differ from their current market value, particularly for long-lived assets recorded net of accumulated depreciation. **Key highlights**

- Assets represent resources controlled by the company.

- They include cash, receivables, inventory and fixed assets.

- They are classified by expected conversion or use period.

#### 6.2.4 Current assets

**Summary:** Definition: Current assets are assets expected to be converted into cash, sold or used within one year. Overview: CFI places cash and equivalents first, followed by accounts receivable and inventory. These assets support short-term operations and are relevant to liquidity analysis. The classification can depend on the company's operating cycle where that cycle is longer than one year. **Key highlights**

- Cash and equivalents are the most liquid current assets.

- Accounts receivable represents credit sales not yet collected.

- Inventory includes raw materials, work in progress and finished goods.

#### 6.2.5 Non-current assets

**Summary:** Definition: Non-current assets are assets held for more than one year. Overview: CFI includes property, plant and equipment (PP&E) and intangible assets in this category. PP&E is reported net of accumulated depreciation, while land is not depreciated. Intangible assets can include identifiable items such as patents and licences, as well as unidentifiable items such as goodwill. **Key highlights**

- PP&E is tangible, long-lived operating property.

- Depreciation reduces the reported carrying amount of depreciable PP&E.

- Intangible assets include items such as patents, licences and goodwill.

#### 6.2.6 Liabilities

**Summary:** Definition: Liabilities are obligations a company owes to other parties. Overview: CFI gives loans and accounts payable as examples. Liabilities are divided into current liabilities, due within one year, and non-current liabilities, due later. They represent claims on the company's assets before shareholders' residual claim. **Key highlights**

- Liabilities are obligations to external parties.

- They include trade payables and borrowings.

- They are classified by expected payment timing.

#### 6.2.7 Current liabilities

**Summary:** Definition: Current liabilities are obligations due within one year or within the company's operating cycle, if longer. Overview: CFI includes accounts payable, current debt or notes payable, and the current portion of long-term debt. Accounts payable represents amounts owed to suppliers for credit purchases. The current portion of long-term debt is the part of a longer loan that must be repaid in the coming year. **Key highlights**

- Accounts payable arises from purchases on credit.

- Current debt must be repaid within the short term.

- Part of long-term debt can become a current liability as repayment approaches.

#### 6.2.8 Non-current liabilities

**Summary:** Definition: Non-current liabilities are obligations due after more than one year. Overview: CFI includes bonds payable and long-term debt in this group. Long-term debt excludes the portion classified as current. These obligations affect a company's leverage and future cash commitments. **Key highlights**

- Long-term debt matures beyond one year.

- Bonds payable are a form of long-term borrowing.

- They affect leverage and future financing needs.

#### 6.2.9 Equity

**Summary:** Definition: Equity is the owners' residual interest in the company after liabilities are deducted from assets. Overview: CFI identifies share capital and retained earnings as major equity components. Share capital represents funds invested by shareholders, while retained earnings represent accumulated net income kept in the company. Dividends reduce retained earnings when paid. **Key highlights**

- Equity = Assets - Liabilities.

- Share capital reflects owner investment.

- Retained earnings reflect accumulated profit kept in the business.

#### 6.2.10 Retained earnings

**Summary:** Definition: Retained earnings are cumulative net income kept by the company rather than distributed as dividends. Overview: CFI explains that each period's net income may increase retained earnings, while dividends reduce them. Retained earnings can therefore become negative if accumulated losses or distributions exceed prior retained profits. The account links the income statement to the balance sheet. **Key highlights**

- Retained earnings increase with net income kept in the business.

- Dividends reduce retained earnings.

- They connect current and prior-period income statements to equity.

#### 6.2.11 Liquidity ordering

**Summary:** Definition: Liquidity ordering places more easily convertible assets first on the balance sheet. Overview: CFI says cash, inventory and trade payables are placed in the current section before less liquid items such as PP&E and long-term debt. This presentation helps readers quickly see resources available in the short term and obligations due soon. The exact ordering can vary by company and reporting framework. **Key highlights**

- Cash is usually shown first among assets.

- Current items precede non-current items.

- Ordering highlights short-term resources and obligations.

#### 6.2.12 Current ratio and debt-to-equity ratio

**Summary:** Definition: The current ratio compares current assets with current liabilities, and debt-to-equity compares debt with shareholders' equity. Overview: CFI classifies the current ratio as a liquidity ratio and debt-to-equity as a leverage ratio. The current ratio indicates whether short-term assets appear sufficient to cover short-term obligations. Debt-to-equity indicates the extent to which the company relies on borrowing relative to owners' equity. **Key highlights**

- Current ratio = Current assets / Current liabilities.

- Debt-to-equity compares debt with equity.

- Ratios support comparison, but must be interpreted in context.

## Source 6.3 — Corporate Finance Institute: *Statement of Cash Flows*

*Jeff Schmidt, 2023, https://corporatefinanceinstitute.com/resources/accounting/statement-of-cash-flows/, accessed 22 November 2026.*

**Status:** Read in full (by the research assistant); the editor confirmed from search results that the CFI page covers operating, investing and financing sections and the direct (rarely used) and indirect (most common) methods.

**Whole-source summary.** CFI explains that the statement of cash flows reports cash and cash-equivalent movements over a period and links the income statement to the balance sheet. It divides cash flows into operating, investing and financing activities, then calculates the net change in cash and the closing cash balance. The article explains the direct and indirect methods, the add-back of depreciation and amortization, and the effect of working-capital changes. It also explains that accrual-based income and balance-sheet figures do not directly measure cash movement.

**Key points**

- The statement covers a period and shows cash inflows and outflows.

- Operating activities relate to the principal revenue-producing activities of the business.

- Investing activities include buying and selling long-term assets and investments.

- Financing activities include borrowing, repaying debt, issuing or repurchasing shares and paying dividends.

- The indirect method begins with profit and adjusts for non-cash items and working-capital changes.

- The ending cash balance must equal the cash balance reported on the balance sheet.

#### 6.3.1 Cash flow statement

**Summary:** Definition: The cash flow statement reports cash received and cash paid during a period. Overview: It shows how a company generated and used cash across operating, investing and financing activities. It bridges the income statement and balance sheet by explaining why the cash balance changed. It is particularly useful for assessing liquidity and whether reported earnings are supported by cash generation. **Key highlights**

- It covers a period, not a single date.

- It tracks cash and cash equivalents.

- It explains the change in the cash balance.

#### 6.3.2 Operating activities

**Summary:** Definition: Operating activities are the principal revenue-producing activities of a business, plus other activities that are neither investing nor financing activities. Overview: This section includes cash from sales and cash paid for operating costs such as rent, salaries and administration. Under the indirect method, it begins with net income and adjusts for non-cash expenses and working-capital movements. It is the section most directly connected with day-to-day business operations. **Key highlights**

- It includes cash from sales and operating payments.

- It is adjusted for non-cash items under the indirect method.

- It can be compared with net income to assess earnings quality.

#### 6.3.3 Investing activities

**Summary:** Definition: Investing activities are cash flows from acquiring or disposing of long-term assets and other investments not classified as cash equivalents. Overview: CFI includes purchases and sales of property, plant and equipment, other non-current assets and financial assets. Cash spent on PP&E is called capital expenditure, or CapEx, and is a cash outflow. Selling such assets generates a cash inflow. **Key highlights**

- CapEx is cash spent on long-lived operating assets.

- Asset sales produce investing cash inflows.

- This section concerns long-term resources, not daily operating payments.

#### 6.3.4 Financing activities

**Summary:** Definition: Financing activities are cash flows arising from changes in a company's capital structure. Overview: CFI includes borrowing and repaying loans or bonds, issuing or buying back shares, and paying dividends. Debt issuance and equity issuance bring cash into the company, while repayments, buybacks and dividends use cash. This section shows how the company funds itself and returns capital to providers of finance. **Key highlights**

- Debt issuance is a cash inflow; repayment is an outflow.

- Share issuance is a cash inflow; buybacks are an outflow.

- Dividends paid are financing outflows in the usual presentation (IFRS allows a choice, see 6.4).

#### 6.3.5 Direct method

**Summary:** Definition: The direct method presents major classes of gross cash receipts and gross cash payments from operating activities. Overview: CFI describes it as a straightforward presentation of cash received from customers and cash paid for operating expenses. It is less commonly used than the indirect method. It gives a more immediate view of operating cash receipts and payments, but it requires more detailed cash-transaction information. **Key highlights**

- It lists major operating cash receipts and payments.

- It shows cash flows directly rather than reconciling from profit.

- It is less commonly used than the indirect method.

#### 6.3.6 Indirect method

**Summary:** Definition: The indirect method starts with profit and adjusts it for non-cash items, accruals, deferrals, and investing or financing-related income and expenses. Overview: CFI says most companies use this method. Depreciation and amortization are added back because they reduced profit without requiring cash payment. Changes in receivables, inventory and payables are then adjusted to reflect the timing difference between accounting recognition and cash movement. **Key highlights**

- It reconciles profit to operating cash flow.

- Depreciation and amortization are added back.

- Working-capital changes adjust for cash-timing differences.

#### 6.3.7 Depreciation add-back

**Summary:** Definition: A depreciation add-back reverses the effect of depreciation expense when calculating operating cash flow under the indirect method. Overview: Depreciation spreads the cost of a tangible long-lived asset over its useful life and reduces net income, but it does not itself require a cash payment. Amortization does the same for certain intangible assets. Adding these expenses back prevents them from being treated as cash outflows in the operating section. **Key highlights**

- Depreciation reduces accounting profit but not cash directly.

- It is added back in the indirect operating section.

- The related cash outflow occurs when the asset is purchased, usually in investing activities.

#### 6.3.8 Working-capital changes

**Summary:** Definition: Working-capital changes are movements in current assets other than cash and current liabilities other than debt that affect operating cash flow. Overview: CFI explains that an increase in inventory or accounts receivable reduces operating cash flow because cash is tied up or not yet collected. An increase in accounts payable increases operating cash flow because the company has not yet paid suppliers. These adjustments convert accrual-based profit into cash-based operating performance. **Key highlights**

- Higher receivables or inventory generally reduce operating cash flow.

- Higher payables generally increase operating cash flow.

- Working capital links the balance sheet to operating cash flow.

#### 6.3.9 Net income is not cash

**Summary:** Definition: Net income is accounting profit under accrual rules, while cash flow is the actual movement of cash and cash equivalents. Overview: CFI emphasizes that income-statement and balance-sheet figures are accrual-based, so they do not directly measure cash movement. A company can report positive net income while cash flow is lower because sales were made on credit, inventory increased or expenses were accrued. The cash flow statement adjusts for these timing and non-cash differences. **Key highlights**

- Net income can include non-cash revenue or expenses.

- Credit sales increase revenue before cash is received.

- The cash flow statement reconciles profit with actual cash movement.

#### 6.3.10 Free cash flow

**Summary:** Definition: Free cash flow is cash generated by operations after investment in long-lived operating assets. Overview: CFI identifies capital expenditure as the main investing outflow for many companies. A common practical calculation is operating cash flow minus capital expenditure. It indicates cash available after maintaining or expanding the asset base, before considering financing decisions. **Key highlights**

- Free cash flow = Operating cash flow - Capital expenditure.

- It focuses on cash after investment in productive assets.

- It is not a line item required on the face of the cash flow statement, and definitions vary in practice.

#### 6.3.11 Ending cash balance

**Summary:** Definition: The ending cash balance is the cash and cash-equivalent balance at the end of the reporting period. Overview: CFI explains that the net change in cash from operating, investing and financing activities is added to the opening cash balance to derive the closing balance. That closing balance is reported in current assets on the balance sheet. This is a central check when linking the three statements. **Key highlights**

- Ending cash = Beginning cash + Net change in cash.

- Net change in cash = CFO + CFI + CFF.

- Ending cash must match balance-sheet cash.

## Source 6.4 — IFRS Foundation, IAS 7: *Statement of Cash Flows*

*IFRS Foundation, International Accounting Standards Board, https://www.ifrs.org/issued-standards/list-of-standards/ias-7-statement-of-cash-flows/ (page confirmed to exist by the editor; the 2026 issued-standards edition is listed), accessed 20 Nov 2026.*

**Status:** Read for the standard's summary and key requirements (by the research assistant); the full standard text was not read clause by clause. Check the current text before relying on the interest and dividend choices described in 6.4.

**Whole-source summary.** IAS 7 requires an entity to prepare a statement of cash flows as an integral part of its financial statements for each period presented. It requires cash flows to be classified as operating, investing or financing activities. It permits either the direct method, showing major classes of gross cash receipts and payments, or the indirect method, adjusting profit or loss for non-cash items, accruals, deferrals, and investing or financing income and expenses. It also requires disclosure of cash and cash-equivalent components and reconciliation with the statement of financial position.

**Key points**

- IAS 7 requires a statement of cash flows for each period for which financial statements are presented.

- Cash flows must be classified into operating, investing and financing activities.

- Either the direct or indirect method may be used for operating cash flows.

- Cash includes cash on hand and demand deposits; cash equivalents are short-term, highly liquid investments with insignificant value-change risk.

- Non-cash investing and financing transactions are excluded from the statement but separately disclosed.

- The standard requires reconciliation between cash and cash equivalents in the cash flow statement and the equivalent balance-sheet items.

#### 6.4.1 Cash and cash equivalents

**Summary:** Definition: Cash includes cash on hand and demand deposits, and cash equivalents are short-term, highly liquid investments readily convertible to known cash amounts with insignificant value-change risk. Overview: IAS 7 uses this definition to determine what belongs in the cash flow statement. The distinction matters because not every liquid asset qualifies as a cash equivalent. The standard requires disclosure of the components of cash and cash equivalents. **Key highlights**

- Cash includes physical cash and demand deposits.

- Cash equivalents must be short-term, liquid and low-risk.

- Components must be disclosed.

#### 6.4.2 Classification requirement

**Summary:** Definition: IAS 7 requires cash flows to be classified as operating, investing or financing. Overview: This classification helps users understand whether cash came from core operations, long-term asset decisions or capital-structure changes. It applies to every cash flow reported in the statement. The classification supports comparability across companies using IFRS. **Key highlights**

- Every cash flow must be classified.

- The three categories are operating, investing and financing.

- Classification supports analysis of cash generation and use.

- IFRS gives companies policy choices for interest and dividends: interest and dividends received are classified as operating or investing, and interest and dividends paid as operating or financing, applied consistently (as listed in an eCampusOntario IFRS text; confirm against the current standard).

#### 6.4.3 Direct method under IAS 7

**Summary:** Definition: Under IAS 7, the direct method discloses major classes of gross cash receipts and gross cash payments. Overview: This approach presents operating cash flows directly, such as cash received from customers and cash paid to suppliers or employees. It provides a clear view of the sources and uses of operating cash. IAS 7 permits this method alongside the indirect method. **Key highlights**

- It shows gross operating cash receipts and payments.

- It presents operating cash flows directly.

- IAS 7 permits its use.

#### 6.4.4 Indirect method under IAS 7

**Summary:** Definition: Under IAS 7, the indirect method adjusts profit or loss for non-cash transactions, deferrals, accruals, and investing or financing income and expenses. Overview: This method begins with an accrual-based profit measure and reconciles it to operating cash flow. It is widely used because companies already maintain accrual-based income and balance-sheet data. The adjustments make visible the difference between reported profit and cash generated by operations. **Key highlights**

- It starts from profit or loss.

- It adjusts for non-cash and timing differences.

- It reconciles accrual profit to operating cash flow.

#### 6.4.5 Reconciliation to the balance sheet

**Summary:** Definition: IAS 7 requires companies to reconcile the cash and cash equivalents in the cash flow statement with the equivalent items in the statement of financial position. Overview: This requirement creates a direct link between the closing cash balance in the cash flow statement and the balance sheet. It helps ensure that the statement explains the change in reported cash. It is a practical control for three-statement models. **Key highlights**

- Cash flow statement cash must reconcile to balance-sheet cash.

- The reconciliation covers cash and cash equivalents.

- It supports the three-statement linkage.

## Source 6.5 — Investopedia: *The Three Major Financial Statements: How They're Interconnected*

**Whole-source summary.** Investopedia explains that the income statement, balance sheet and cash flow statement provide complementary views of a company. The income statement shows profitability over a period; the balance sheet shows assets, liabilities and equity at a date; and the cash flow statement tracks cash movements over a period. It explains that net income flows into retained earnings and begins the indirect operating cash flow calculation. It also identifies common ratios used with each statement.

**Key points**

- The three statements together provide a fuller view of financial health than any one statement alone.

- Net income becomes retained earnings on the balance sheet, adjusted for dividends.

- Net income is the starting point for indirect operating cash flow.

- Sales recognized on credit create accounts receivable on the balance sheet.

- Operating cash flow includes cash from sales and payments for operating costs.

- Investing and financing cash flows relate to assets and capital structure.

#### 6.5.1 Three-statement linkage

**Summary:** Definition: Three-statement linkage is the set of connections through which income, balance-sheet and cash-flow figures affect one another. Overview: Net income from the income statement increases retained earnings, subject to dividends. It also starts the indirect operating cash flow calculation. Changes in balance-sheet accounts, such as receivables, inventory, payables, PP&E, debt and equity, drive cash-flow adjustments. **Key highlights**

- Net income links the income statement to equity and cash flow.

- Balance-sheet changes drive cash-flow adjustments.

- The statements must reconcile for a model to be internally consistent.

#### 6.5.2 Net income to retained earnings

**Summary:** Definition: Net income increases retained earnings, while dividends reduce retained earnings. Overview: Investopedia states that net income at the end of a period becomes part of shareholders' equity as retained earnings. If the company pays dividends, the retained-earnings increase is smaller than net income. This link explains why a profitable company may still have limited retained earnings if it distributes much of its profit. **Key highlights**

- Retained earnings rise with profit kept in the business.

- Dividends reduce retained earnings.

- The link runs from the income statement to equity.

#### 6.5.3 Net income to operating cash flow

**Summary:** Definition: Under the indirect method, net income is the starting point for calculating cash flow from operations. Overview: Investopedia explains that net income is carried to the cash flow statement as the top line for operating activities. Adjustments are then made for non-cash expenses and working-capital changes. This produces cash generated by operations rather than accounting profit alone. **Key highlights**

- Net income begins the indirect operating section.

- Non-cash expenses are added back.

- Working-capital changes are adjusted.

#### 6.5.4 Accounts receivable link

**Summary:** Definition: Accounts receivable records sales made on credit that have not yet been collected in cash. Overview: Investopedia notes that sales booked during a period are added to short-term assets as accounts receivable. Revenue can therefore rise before cash is received. When the customer later pays, accounts receivable falls and cash rises. **Key highlights**

- Credit sales create receivables.

- Receivables represent cash not yet collected.

- Collection converts receivables into cash.
