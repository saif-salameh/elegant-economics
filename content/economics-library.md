## Source 1.1 — *Introduction to Economics* (university module, September 2019)

**Whole-source summary.** A six-chapter teaching module that moves from what economics is (scarcity, choice, economic systems) through microeconomics (demand and supply, consumer behaviour, production and cost, market structures) to macroeconomics (national income, price indices, business cycles, unemployment, inflation, policy). Its running thread is that unlimited wants meet scarce resources, so every society must choose what, how, and for whom to produce. Examples and numbers use Ethiopia and the birr, so they would need adapting for a Jordan-focused site.

**Key points**

- Economics is the study of allocating scarce resources efficiently to satisfy unlimited wants.

- Microeconomics covers individual units (households, firms, markets); macroeconomics covers economy-wide aggregates.

- Price is set where demand meets supply; elasticity measures how responsive buyers and sellers are.

- Firms decide using production and cost curves, and market structure determines pricing power.

- Macro performance is tracked with GDP/GNP, price indices, unemployment, and inflation, and governments steer it with monetary and fiscal policy.

**Chapter map:** Ch. 1 Basics of Economics · Ch. 2 Demand and Supply · Ch. 3 Consumer Behaviour · Ch. 4 Production and Cost · Ch. 5 Market Structure · Ch. 6 Fundamental Concepts of Macroeconomics.

### Chapter 1 — Basics of Economics

#### 1.1.1 Definition of economics

**Summary:** Economics is the social science of allocating scarce resources efficiently to satisfy unlimited human needs. The word comes from the Greek for managing a household, and the subject became distinct with Adam Smith's *Wealth of Nations* (1776). The module says no universally accepted definition exists. **Key highlights**

- Four definitional lenses: wealth, welfare, scarcity, growth.

- Called a "science of choice".

- Objective: maximum fulfilment of wants through efficient allocation.

#### 1.1.2 The rationale for studying economics

**Summary:** Two facts underpin the whole field: human material wants are unlimited, and economic resources are limited. Economists study the choices this forces on individuals, families, and nations. **Key highlights**

- Choice sits at the heart of all decision-making.

- Outcomes are judged by efficiency, equity, and stability.

- Economists also look for forms of economic organization that raise living standards or spread well-being more fairly.

#### 1.1.3 Microeconomics vs macroeconomics

**Summary:** Microeconomics analyzes individual decision-making units and how they interact in specific markets; macroeconomics analyzes the aggregate behaviour of the whole economy. The two are complementary, and macro cannot be studied in isolation from micro. **Key highlights**

- Micro: individual prices, outputs, and incomes; main tools are demand and supply of particular goods.

- Macro: national income, general price level, employment; main tools are aggregate demand and supply.

- Newer branches include development, environmental, and welfare economics.

#### 1.1.4 Positive vs normative analysis

**Summary:** Positive economics describes what was, is, or will be and can be checked against facts. Normative economics says what ought to be and rests on value judgments. **Key highlights**

- "Inflation is 12 percent" is positive; "the poor should pay no taxes" is normative.

- Positive disagreements are settled with evidence; normative ones are matters of opinion.

- Useful framing for any site section that mixes facts with policy opinion.

#### 1.1.5 Inductive vs deductive reasoning

**Summary:** A theory is a simplified picture of reality. Inductive reasoning builds general theories from specific observations; deductive reasoning starts from assumptions or axioms and derives specific conclusions, which are then tested against facts. **Key highlights**

- Inductive steps: select a problem, collect and analyze data, establish cause and effect.

- Deductive steps: identify the problem, state assumptions, form hypotheses, test them.

#### 1.1.6 Scarcity and economic resources

**Summary:** Scarcity is the imbalance between unlimited wants and finite means. A free resource exists in more than the desired amount at zero price (sunshine); an economic resource is scarce. Scarcity is permanent and universal, unlike a shortage, which is a short-term, specific problem at the going price. **Key highlights**

- Four factors of production and their rewards: labour (wage), land (rent), capital (interest), entrepreneurship (profit).

- Entrepreneurs organize production, make policy decisions, innovate, and bear risk of loss.

- Scarcity does not mean shortage.

#### 1.1.7 Choice and opportunity cost

**Summary:** Scarcity forces choice, and every choice gives up an alternative. Opportunity cost is the value of the next best alternative sacrificed to get one more unit of something. **Key highlights**

- Chain: scarcity → limited output → choice → opportunity cost.

- Measured in goods and services forgone, not money.

- When an activity's opportunity cost rises, people substitute away from it.

#### 1.1.8 Production possibilities frontier (PPF) and economic growth

**Summary:** The PPF shows the combinations of goods a society can produce with given resources and technology. Points on the curve are efficient, points inside are attainable but inefficient, and points outside are unattainable. Growth appears as an outward shift of the curve. **Key highlights**

- Illustrates scarcity, choice, and opportunity cost in one picture.

- Law of increasing opportunity cost makes the PPF concave, because resources are not equally adaptable to every use.

- Growth comes from more or better resources, or better technology; gains in one sector alone cause asymmetric growth.

#### 1.1.9 The three basic economic questions

**Summary:** Every economy must decide what to produce, how to produce it, and for whom. These are the central problems that economic systems exist to answer. **Key highlights**

- What: allocation of resources (consumer vs capital goods, civil vs military, necessities vs luxuries).

- How: choice of technique, labour-intensive or capital-intensive, depending on factor supplies and relative prices.

- For whom: distribution of the national product.

#### 1.1.10 Capitalist (market) economy

**Summary:** Means of production are privately owned, activity is driven by private profit, and government intervention is minimal. The price mechanism answers the basic questions. **Key highlights**

- Features: private property, consumer sovereignty, profit motive, competition, self-interest.

- Strengths: flexibility, entrepreneurship, higher per-capita income, capital formation.

- Weaknesses: income inequality, unbalanced development, labour exploitation, negative externalities such as pollution.

#### 1.1.11 Command (socialist) economy

**Summary:** The state owns and controls production and distribution, with central planning allocating resources toward social goals. **Key highlights**

- Features: collective ownership, central planning, strong government role, relative income equality.

- Strengths: no wasteful competition, balanced regional growth, fewer private monopolies.

- Weaknesses: no automatic price mechanism, weak incentives, little economic freedom, red tape.

#### 1.1.12 Mixed economy

**Summary:** Public and private sectors coexist, aiming to combine the benefits of both systems. Strategic industries tend to be public, consumer and small-scale industry private. **Key highlights**

- Price mechanism works in the private sector; the government regulates essential goods and prices.

- Tools for equality: progressive taxation, subsidies, limits on property holdings.

- Risks: inefficiency in both sectors, economic fluctuations, corruption, and black markets if rules are weakly enforced.

#### 1.1.13 Decision-making units and the circular flow model

**Summary:** In a closed economy, households, firms, and government are the decision-makers. They meet in the product market (goods and services) and the factor market (inputs). The circular flow diagram shows resources and goods moving one way and money moving the other. **Key highlights**

- Households sell resources and buy goods; firms buy resources and sell goods.

- Two-sector model: household income equals firms' spending on factors; firm revenue equals household consumption.

- Government provides public goods and can influence households, firms, and markets.

### Chapter 2 — Theory of Demand and Supply

#### 1.1.14 Demand and the law of demand

**Summary:** In economics, demand means desire backed by purchasing power: the buyer must be both willing and able. The law of demand says that, other things equal, quantity demanded rises as price falls. **Key highlights**

- Shown as a schedule, a downward-sloping curve, or a function$Q_{d}\  = \ f(P)$.

- Market demand is the horizontal sum of individual demands.

- *Ceteris paribus* (other things equal) is the key assumption.

#### 1.1.15 Determinants of demand and shifts of the curve

**Summary:** A price change moves you along the demand curve; a change in any other determinant shifts the whole curve. The shifters are tastes, income, prices of related goods, expectations, and the number of buyers. **Key highlights**

- Normal goods: demand rises with income. Inferior goods: demand falls with income.

- Substitutes: price of one moves demand for the other in the same direction. Complements: opposite direction.

- The summary also notes Giffen goods as an exception to the usual demand pattern.

#### 1.1.16 Elasticity of demand

**Summary:** Elasticity measures how strongly quantity demanded responds to a change in price (price elasticity), income (income elasticity), or another good's price (cross elasticity). It is a ratio of percentage changes, so it is unit-free. **Key highlights**

- Price elasticity above 1 is elastic (luxury-like); between 0 and 1 is inelastic (necessity-like); equal to 1 is unitary.

- Drivers: availability of substitutes, necessity vs luxury, share of income spent, and time (more elastic in the long run).

- Cross elasticity is positive for substitutes, negative for complements, and zero for unrelated goods; negative income elasticity marks an inferior good.

#### 1.1.17 Supply and the law of supply

**Summary:** Supply is the quantity producers are willing and able to offer at each price. The law of supply says that, other things equal, quantity supplied rises with price. **Key highlights**

- Determinants: input prices, technology, prices of related products, taxes and subsidies, expectations, and number of sellers (weather matters for agriculture).

- Cheaper inputs or better technology shift supply outward.

- Market supply is the horizontal sum of individual supplies.

#### 1.1.18 Elasticity of supply

**Summary:** Supply elasticity measures how responsive quantity supplied is to price. Supply can be elastic, inelastic, unitary, perfectly elastic (horizontal), or perfectly inelastic (vertical). **Key highlights**

- Elastic supply: a small price change causes a large change in output.

- The point method is the one most commonly used.

#### 1.1.19 Market equilibrium

**Summary:** Equilibrium is where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. Above that price there is a surplus; below it, a shortage. **Key highlights**

- Higher demand raises both price and quantity; higher supply lowers price and raises quantity.

- When demand and supply both change, the effect on quantity is clear but the effect on price depends on which shift is larger.

- The module works through numerical equilibrium examples.

### Chapter 3 — Theory of Consumer Behaviour

#### 1.1.20 Consumer preferences and utility

**Summary:** Consumers compare bundles of goods and judge one strictly better, or are indifferent. Utility is the satisfaction a good provides, or its power to satisfy wants, and it is subjective and consumer-centred. **Key highlights**

- Preference relations: strict preference, weak preference, indifference.

- Utility differs from person to person.

- Two ways to compare utility: cardinal and ordinal.

#### 1.1.21 Cardinal utility theory and diminishing marginal utility

**Summary:** The cardinal school holds that utility can be measured in units. Total utility is overall satisfaction; marginal utility is the extra satisfaction from one more unit. The law of diminishing marginal utility says each additional unit adds less. **Key highlights**

- A consumer with one good is in equilibrium where marginal utility equals price; with several goods, where MU/price is equal across goods.

- Assumes constant marginal utility of money and additive utility.

- Limitations: utility is hard to quantify, and the marginal utility of money is not really constant.

#### 1.1.22 Ordinal utility and indifference curves

**Summary:** The ordinal school says utility cannot be measured but can be ranked. An indifference curve shows all combinations of two goods giving the same satisfaction, and a set of them forms an indifference map. **Key highlights**

- Properties: downward-sloping, convex to the origin, higher curves preferred, curves do not intersect.

- Convexity reflects a diminishing marginal rate of substitution.

- Assumes consistent (transitive) preferences.

#### 1.1.23 Budget line and consumer equilibrium

**Summary:** The budget line shows every combination of two goods the consumer can afford given prices and income. The consumer maximizes utility where the budget line is tangent to the highest attainable indifference curve. **Key highlights**

- Combines what the consumer wants (indifference curves) with what they can afford (budget line).

- The ordinal approach avoids the measurement problem of the cardinal approach.

### Chapter 4 — Theory of Production and Cost

#### 1.1.24 Production and the production function

**Summary:** Production transforms inputs into outputs that have exchange value. The production function is the technical relationship between inputs and the maximum output possible with existing technology. Fixed inputs cannot be changed quickly; variable inputs can, and the short run is the period in which at least one input is fixed. **Key highlights**

- Inputs: land, labour, capital, entrepreneurial ability.

- Typical short-run setup: capital fixed, labour variable.

- "Short run" is defined by the fixed input, not by a set number of months.

#### 1.1.25 Total, average, and marginal product; the law of variable proportions

**Summary:** Total product is overall output, average product is output per unit of the variable input, and marginal product is the extra output from one more unit. The law of variable proportions (diminishing returns) says that beyond some point each added unit of the variable input adds less output. **Key highlights**

- Marginal product first rises, peaks, then falls; it measures the slope of total product.

- The law assumes technology is fixed.

#### 1.1.26 Stages of short-run production

**Summary:** Short-run production splits into three stages. The efficient one is Stage II, where marginal product is positive but declining. **Key highlights**

- Stage I: average product rising, fixed input under-used, not efficient.

- Stage II: diminishing marginal returns, the efficient region.

- Stage III: further input additions reduce total output.

#### 1.1.27 Types of cost: explicit, implicit, accounting, economic

**Summary:** Explicit costs are out-of-pocket payments for purchased inputs; implicit costs are the estimated value of self-owned inputs. Accounting cost counts only explicit costs; economic cost counts both. **Key highlights**

- Accounting profit = revenue − explicit cost; economic profit = revenue − (explicit + implicit cost).

- With implicit costs present, accounting profit exceeds economic profit by that amount.

#### 1.1.28 Short-run cost curves

**Summary:** Total cost splits into fixed cost (unavoidable at any output) and variable cost (rises with output). From these come per-unit curves: average fixed, average variable, average total, and marginal cost. **Key highlights**

- Average variable and average total cost are U-shaped because of the law of variable proportions.

- Marginal cost mirrors marginal product, and average variable cost mirrors average product.

- A firm can avoid fixed costs only by shutting down.

### Chapter 5 — Market Structure

#### 1.1.29 Markets in physical and digital space

**Summary:** A market is any place or digital space where goods, services, and ideas are exchanged. The module contrasts physical markets, where buyers and sellers meet in person, with digital channels that carry product value from producer to consumer over networks. **Key highlights**

- Digital marketing uses the internet, mobile phones, display advertising, and other digital media.

- Useful bridge to the digital and gig economy concepts in Sources 1.2 and 3.1.

#### 1.1.30 Perfectly competitive market

**Summary:** A market with so many buyers and sellers that none can influence price, so every firm is a price taker. The firm maximizes profit where marginal revenue equals marginal cost, and in this market price equals marginal revenue. **Key highlights**

- Five assumptions: many buyers and sellers, homogeneous product, perfect factor mobility, free entry and exit, perfect market knowledge.

- In the short run a firm can earn economic profit, normal (zero) profit, or a loss.

- Shutdown point: price equals minimum average variable cost; the industry is in equilibrium when the market clears.

#### 1.1.31 Monopoly

**Summary:** A single seller with no close substitutes and blocked entry. Because it faces the whole downward-sloping market demand, it is a price maker. **Key highlights**

- Barriers to entry are the source of monopoly power.

- Four sources: legal restriction (e.g., public utilities), control of key raw materials, efficiency and economies of scale (natural monopoly), and patent rights.

#### 1.1.32 Monopolistic competition

**Summary:** A blend of competition and monopoly: many firms sell differentiated products and entry is easy. Differentiation (style, brand, quality) gives each seller limited power over its loyal customers. **Key highlights**

- Competitive elements: many firms, no entry barriers.

- Monopoly element: differentiated products, so some control over price within narrow limits.

- The module separates real from fancied (perceived) differentiation.

#### 1.1.33 Oligopoly and comparing market structures

**Summary:** A few large firms dominate, and each is affected by its rivals' price and output decisions. Because they fear price wars, firms lean on non-price competition such as advertising, warranties, and after-sales service. **Key highlights**

- Defining feature is interdependence; entry barriers are considerable; products may be homogeneous or differentiated; a two-firm market is a duopoly.

- Comparison from the module:

| Structure                | Firms     | Product                       | Price control                            | Entry                 | Examples              |
|--------------------------|-----------|-------------------------------|------------------------------------------|-----------------------|-----------------------|
| Pure competition         | Very many | Homogeneous                   | None                                     | Very easy             | Agricultural products |
| Monopolistic competition | Many      | Differentiated                | Some, narrow                             | Relatively easy       | Clothes, shoes        |
| Oligopoly                | Few       | Homogeneous or differentiated | Limited by interdependence and collusion | Considerable barriers | Steel, automobiles    |
| Pure monopoly            | One       | Unique, no close substitutes  | Significant                              | Blocked               | Local utilities       |

### Chapter 6 — Fundamental Concepts of Macroeconomics

#### 1.1.34 Goals of macroeconomics

**Summary:** Macroeconomics studies the economy's aggregate behaviour: growth, employment, inflation, income distribution, macro policy, and international trade. The goals revolve around full employment, stable prices, and sustainable growth, along with managing budget and balance-of-payments deficits. **Key highlights**

- Performance is judged at the whole-economy level.

- The ultimate policy objective is sustainable growth and development.

#### 1.1.35 National income accounting: GDP and GNP

**Summary:** National income accounting measures aggregate output and income using the circular flow idea. GDP counts output produced within a country; GNP adds net factor income from abroad (GNP = GDP + NFI). **Key highlights**

- Three ways to measure: product (value-added), expenditure, and income approaches.

- If net foreign income is positive, GNP exceeds GDP.

- Related accounts such as net national product (NNP) subtract depreciation.

#### 1.1.36 Nominal vs real GDP

**Summary:** Nominal GDP values a year's output at that year's prices, so it can rise from higher prices alone. Real GDP values output at base-year prices, removing the inflation effect. **Key highlights**

- Real GDP is the better gauge of actual output growth.

- The module's worked example shows nominal GDP growing far faster than real GDP when prices rise.

#### 1.1.37 GDP deflator and Consumer Price Index (CPI)

**Summary:** The GDP deflator is nominal GDP divided by real GDP (×100), a price measure for all domestically produced goods. The CPI tracks the cost of a fixed basket of consumer goods relative to a base year. **Key highlights**

- Deflator covers all domestic output; CPI covers what consumers buy, including imports.

- CPI uses fixed weights; the deflator's weights change as output changes.

- Both are used to measure inflation.

#### 1.1.38 The business cycle

**Summary:** The business cycle is the recurring pattern of ups and downs in economic activity: peak (boom), recession, trough, and recovery. A full cycle runs from one peak to the next or one trough to the next. **Key highlights**

- Cycles vary in duration and intensity.

- Booms see output growing faster than potential; recessions bring falling output and rising unemployment; a severe one reaches depression.

- Fluctuate around a long-term trend path of GDP.

#### 1.1.39 Unemployment

**Summary:** The unemployed are people in the labour force who have no job but are actively searching. The unemployment rate is total unemployed divided by the labour force. **Key highlights**

- Types: frictional (short transitions), structural (skills or location mismatch), cyclical (too few vacancies in a downturn).

- Natural rate = frictional + structural; cyclical is the part above it.

- At the natural rate the economy is at full employment.

#### 1.1.40 Inflation

**Summary:** Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level, measured as the percentage change in a price index such as the CPI. The module sorts causes into demand-pull and cost-push. **Key highlights**

- Demand-pull: total demand outpaces supply. Cost-push: falling aggregate supply from higher costs.

- Nominal interest rate = real rate + inflation rate.

- Effects: higher nominal rates, reduced investment, uncertainty, and redistribution of income.

#### 1.1.41 Trade deficit and budget deficit

**Summary:** A budget deficit occurs when government spending exceeds tax revenue; it is financed by borrowing, and accumulated borrowing is government debt. A trade deficit means imports exceed exports, which (via S − I = NX) means the country is a net borrower in world financial markets. **Key highlights**

- Saving minus investment equals net exports (the trade balance).

- The module's Ethiopian case: deficits financed mainly by concessional external loans, with non-concessional loans reserved for state-enterprise projects.

- Useful link to sovereign default (Source 3.1).

#### 1.1.42 Monetary policy

**Summary:** Control of the money supply, credit, and interest rates to steer aggregate demand, usually run by the central bank. It is a flexible stabilization tool. **Key highlights**

- In a recession, it aims to raise demand; in a boom, it offsets inflation pressure.

- Lower interest rates push firms to borrow and invest more; higher rates do the reverse.

- Investment and saving decisions respond to the real interest rate.

#### 1.1.43 Fiscal policy

**Summary:** The use of government spending, taxation, and borrowing to influence activity, aggregate demand, output, and employment. It affects both aggregate demand and aggregate supply. **Key highlights**

- Most governments use it to promote stable growth while also reducing poverty through redistribution.

- Effects can be short-term and long-term.

- Pairs with monetary policy as the two main macro policy instruments.

## Source 1.2 — FutureLearn: *How does the economy work? A quick guide to the basics of economics*

*Blog explainer by "Fran", published June 2021, last modified December 2024.*

**Whole-source summary.** An introductory explainer that defines a set of core terms, describes economies at local, national, and global scale, compares economic systems and market types, mentions green, digital, and gig economies, and then explains how an economy forms and works and how it touches daily life. Its central idea is that an economy distributes scarce resources through networks of markets, with different levels of government involvement. It is also a course-promotion page, so it stays at a high level and links out for depth.

**Key points**

- An economy is a web of production and consumption that allocates resources and capital.

- Economies nest at three scales (local, national, global) and are increasingly linked by globalisation.

- Systems differ by who owns production and how prices are set, and real economies are rarely pure.

- Performance depends on growth, unemployment, inflation, interest and exchange rates, and commodity prices; GDP is the headline measure.

- The economy shapes living costs, jobs, government spending, and quality of life.

#### 1.2.1 Core vocabulary

**Summary:** A fifteen-term glossary the article builds on: boom and bust, budget, capital, debt, economy, economics, financial system, GDP, globalisation, growth, inflation, macroeconomics, microeconomics, recession, stock market. **Key highlights**

- Good seed for the site's glossary page.

- Notes that debt lets governments, companies, and individuals invest beyond what they could afford otherwise.

- Defines the stock market as an interconnected system of businesses, investors, and securities where shares are traded.

#### 1.2.2 Local economies

**Summary:** Interconnected markets and networks within a community, where local businesses, government, and residents trade, pay taxes, and work. Historically, production and consumption happened in the same place until around the 19th century. **Key highlights**

- Local economies still exist but are now part of larger networks.

#### 1.2.3 National economies

**Summary:** Citing the Bank of England, the article describes a national economy as a system for distributing scarce resources: workers, land, and materials are limited while demand is effectively unlimited. **Key highlights**

- Governments shape their economies very differently (the US vs China is the example).

- National economies are themselves parts of a wider network of nations.

#### 1.2.4 The global economy

**Summary:** The economy of all humans, covering activity within and between nations, including exchange of capital and production and consumption of goods. **Key highlights**

- Globalisation, international trade, finance, and investment power it.

#### 1.2.5 Economic systems: feudalism, capitalism, socialism, communism

**Summary:** Feudalism (medieval, land held by a ruler and allocated to nobles), capitalism (private ownership and profit motive, most economies today), socialism (state or public ownership, production by social need), and communism (community ownership, aiming at a classless society). **Key highlights**

- Capitalism: supply and demand set prices; the article's examples are the US, UK, and Australia.

- Socialism examples given: North Korea and Sri Lanka; communism: China, Cuba, Vietnam.

- The article itself notes countries can be both capitalist and communist; these labels are simplified, so verify before publishing.

#### 1.2.6 Market types: command, free, and mixed

**Summary:** Classified by how much the government controls the factors of production. For each, the article answers what, how, and for whom to produce. **Key highlights**

- Command: government preferences decide all three.

- Free: consumers decide what, profit-seeking producers decide how, purchasing power decides for whom.

- Mixed: a blend, with government setting boundaries for the market.

#### 1.2.7 Other types of economy: green, digital, gig

**Summary:** Green economy aims at sustainable development without environmental damage (low-carbon, resource-efficient, inclusive). Digital economy is based on computers and the internet. Gig economy relies on freelancers and short-term contracts. **Key highlights**

- The gig economy offers flexibility but can make career progression harder.

- Green economy links to Source 2.1; gig economy links to Source 3.1.

#### 1.2.8 How an economy forms

**Summary:** Citing Investopedia, an economy forms when people combine skills and interests and trade because they expect to be better off. Households, businesses, government, the product market, and the resource market form its basic structure (the circular flow). **Key highlights**

- Echoes the circular flow model in Source 1.1 (concept 1.1.13).

#### 1.2.9 Macroeconomics and microeconomics

**Summary:** Macro looks at the big picture (employment, GDP, inflation) that governments set fiscal policy around; micro looks at how households and businesses decide, including the effect of supply and demand. **Key highlights**

- Both levels influence how an economy works.

#### 1.2.10 Forces and indicators that shape economic performance

**Summary:** An economy is a network of markets of buyers and sellers allocating scarce resources. How it performs depends on growth, unemployment, inflation, interest and exchange rates, and commodity prices, and success is usually measured by GDP. **Key highlights**

- These factors affect income and purchasing power for households and organizations.

#### 1.2.11 How the economy affects everyday life

**Summary:** Four effects: living costs (tied to inflation), employment (booms create jobs), government spending (more tax revenue funds infrastructure and services), and quality of life (more resources for education and healthcare, "at least in theory"). **Key highlights**

- A fintech executive quoted in the article says tech firms can transform both local and global economies through education access and innovative banking.

- The article calls its treatment simplified; real mechanisms are more complex.

# Category 2 — Modern & Emerging Economic Models

## Source 2.1 — Stošić Mihajlović & Trajković: *Modern Economy: Features and Developments*

*Journal of Process Management – New Technologies, Vol. 4 No. 2, 2016 (Graduate School of Applied Professional Studies, Vranje, Serbia).*

**Whole-source summary.** A short academic paper arguing that economics keeps producing new named "economies" (green or ecological, creative, knowledge) as sciences fragment into specialisms and society must adapt to constant change. It explains the origin, context, definition, and importance of each, and concludes that the modern economy is turning toward nature, creativity, and knowledge, with the "entrepreneurial economy" as the practical face of the knowledge economy. The English is a rough translation, and several factual claims are dated or unsupported (see notes at the end).

**Key points**

- New economic sub-disciplines emerge from specialization and the need to adapt to change; advances in physics, biology, mathematics, and technology gave the impetus.

- The green economy rests on the idea that nature is neither an endless supply nor an endless dump.

- The creative economy rests on creative industries and intellectual property.

- The knowledge economy treats knowledge and human capital as the main growth factor and builds on, rather than replaces, economies of scale.

- The modern era is defined by rapid change, uncertainty, and sustainability requirements.

#### 2.1.1 Why new economic disciplines emerge

**Summary:** Sciences have split into narrow specialisms, and the unused "no man's land" between classical disciplines became the breeding ground for hybrids (biochemistry, environmental economics, green economics). The paper says the deeper cause is society's need to keep adapting to change. **Key highlights**

- Drivers named: modern physics (probability, entropy, information), biology and genetics, mathematics (probability, game theory), and technological development.

- Drucker is cited: organizations must drop yesterday's problems to free resources for new ones, and economics must do the same.

- Cybernetics founder Norbert Wiener is cited on fertile research lying between disciplines.

#### 2.1.2 Green (ecological) economy

**Summary:** Defined (via UNEP) as an economy aiming at human well-being and social justice while reducing environmental impact. The paper places its rise in the late 20th century, when degradation of the environment became a visible threat to health, safety, and jobs. **Key highlights**

- Core rule: operate within the carrying capacity of natural resources; profit pursued at nature's expense eventually backfires.

- Overlapping labels in use: organic economy, green economy, economics of sustainable development.

- Crisis seen as three interacting problems: resource depletion, climate change, and breakdown of the prevailing socio-economic system.

- Paradox noted: the richest regions harm the environment most but hold the fewest people; the problem is global.

- Green economy is described as still a framework rather than a finished set of answers.

#### 2.1.3 Ordoliberalism and the eco-social market economy

**Summary:** The paper favours an optimal mix of market, government, and social control over production, distribution, and consumption. It holds up German ordoliberalism, described as a variant of German social liberalism, and Germany's eco-social market economy as the best-performing European example. **Key highlights**

- Links to the mixed-economy concept (1.1.12) and to the state-vs-market debate.

- Claim of "best results" is the authors' judgment, not tested in the paper.

#### 2.1.4 Creative economy

**Summary:** An economy built on creative industries, which produce goods with cultural and symbolic value from imagination, talent, and skill. The concept is old; what is new is the scale of the link between creativity and economic value. **Key highlights**

- Its proponents are often from applied arts and technical fields rather than economics.

- Scope has widened from music and film to applied arts, software, video games, and marketing.

- Three elements: the creative person, the economic value created, and legal protection of intellectual property.

- Creates new, often well-paid jobs based on skill and talent.

- Definitions range from creative clusters, to professional occupations and industries, to a holistic view of culture, economy, and technology (UNDP 2008).

#### 2.1.5 Knowledge economy vs knowledge-based economy

**Summary:** Knowledge is presented as the most important growth potential, driven by information and communication technology. The paper distinguishes the *knowledge economy* (management and production of knowledge) from the *knowledge-based economy* (applying knowledge through technology in products). **Key highlights**

- Often equated with an economy based on human or intellectual capital.

- Draws on Drucker's distinction between manual workers and knowledge workers.

- Multidisciplinary: economists, technologists, computer scientists, and others.

- Main difficulty: formalizing knowledge and the process of acquiring it.

- Will not replace economies of scale; it is folded into their development.

#### 2.1.6 The entrepreneurial economy and modern-era uncertainty

**Summary:** The conclusion ties the strands together: today's environment changes fast and is highly uncertain, new products reach markets at shorter intervals, and sustainability demands are tightening. The knowledge-based economy is often identified with the entrepreneurial economy. **Key highlights**

- Entrepreneurial economy traits: innovation, improvement consistent with ecology, factor mobility, sustainable-development requirements.

- Human capital and management skills play the key role.

- Frames the whole modern economy as turning toward nature, creativity, and knowledge.

## Source 3.1 — World Economic Forum: *Here's what you need to know about the global economy in 10 articles from the Forum*

*By Tom Crowfoot, Forum Stories, 13 October 2022.*

**Whole-source summary.** A roundup that links ten earlier Forum explainers on key economic terms, written during the 2022 inflation surge. Each entry is a short blurb covering recession risk and inequality, interest rates, recessions, foreign currency reserves, central bank digital currencies, the gig economy, alternatives to GDP, sovereign default, the yield curve, and bear markets. Only the blurbs were read, so each concept below is a pointer to a fuller article, and the figures are 2022-era.

- Central banks raise interest rates to slow price rises, at a cost to borrowers.

- Three "warning signal" concepts: recession, the yield curve, bear markets.

- Three structural shifts: central bank digital currencies, the gig economy, and moving beyond GDP.

#### 3.1.1 Preparing for a new economic reality

**Summary:** The Forum's September 2022 Chief Economists Outlook rated a global recession as somewhat likely, and the OECD warned that indicators had worsened. The cost of living was hitting low-income economies hardest. **Key highlights**

- Forum leadership pointed to growing inequality within and between countries as a legacy of COVID-19, war, and uncoordinated policy.

- Underlying article asks four chief economists how to protect the most vulnerable.

#### 3.1.2 Rising global interest rates

**Summary:** Central banks use interest rates, the price of borrowing, to slow rising prices. Higher rates push up mortgage and credit card repayments. **Key highlights**

- Direct link to inflation (1.1.40) and monetary policy (1.1.42).

#### 3.1.3 What is a recession?

**Summary:** The US National Bureau of Economic Research defines it as a significant decline in activity spread across the economy, lasting more than a few months and visible in production, employment, and real income. It starts at a peak and ends at a trough. **Key highlights**

- Compare with the module's looser business-cycle treatment (1.1.38) and FutureLearn's "negative growth" glossary entry (1.2.1).

#### 3.1.4 Foreign currency (forex) reserves

**Summary:** Cash and other assets such as gold held by central banks or institutions like the IMF. **Key highlights**

- Roles: maintaining liquidity in crises and diversifying a country's financial portfolio.

#### 3.1.5 Central bank digital currencies (CBDCs)

**Summary:** Electronic money issued by a central bank; the European Central Bank describes it as a risk-free form of money guaranteed by the state. The page notes more than 100 countries were exploring them in 2022. **Key highlights**

- Distinct from private crypto-assets because the state backs it.

- The 2022 country count is likely out of date.

#### 3.1.6 The gig economy

**Summary:** Per the UK government, labour is exchanged for money through digital platforms that match providers and customers, on short-term, pay-per-task terms. It offers freedom but raises policy questions about benefits, income security, and training. **Key highlights**

- Overlaps with FutureLearn's gig-economy entry (1.2.7).

#### 3.1.7 Beyond GDP

**Summary:** Many economists consider GDP an outdated measure of well-being, and even its inventor, Simon Kuznets, argued for a new one. The Forum's 2021 post-COVID proposal is a scorecard balancing four dimensions: prosperity, the planet, people, and the role of institutions. **Key highlights**

- Counterpoint to the module's GDP-centred treatment (1.1.35).

#### 3.1.8 Sovereign default

**Summary:** A default occurs when a government cannot or will not meet some or all of its debt payments. The page cites 147 governments defaulting since 1960 (Bank of England data), and notes poor credit ratings make it hard to raise new debt. **Key highlights**

**Summary:** A graph of the returns investors can expect on bonds that mature at different times. Fidelity is cited saying it can help gauge the economy's direction; it is read as a picture of bond investors' feelings about risk. **Key highlights**

# Category 4 — Financial Sector & Financing

*Suggested track: Financing.* How banks and financial markets work, and how they are taxed.

## Source 4.1 — IMF: *Taxing the Financial Sector*, Chapter 1 "Banks" (John Isaac)

*Volume edited by Howell H. Zee, International Monetary Fund, 2004. Contents, preface, and Chapter 1 were provided.*

**Whole-source summary.** Chapter 1 explains how tax systems treat banks. It covers what counts as banking for tax purposes, bank income (from assets, from realizing assets, from fees), the two deductions that raise unique problems (interest payable and bad debts), and selected issues: financial instruments, foreign exchange, double tax relief, branches and subsidiaries of foreign banks, transfer pricing, and turnover taxes. The volume's preface explains why this area is hard: the form of a financial transaction can be changed without changing its economic substance, so loopholes are easy to exploit and neutrality across financial activities is the key goal. Most papers were prepared for an IMF mission to China in 2002 (tied to its WTO accession) but are not China-specific. Only chapter titles are known for the rest of the volume: insurance companies, securities companies/investment and pension funds, innovative financial instruments, financial services and the VAT, and securities transactions.

**Key points**

- Tax practice generally treats as banking whatever the central bank or regulator does, and follows best accounting practice.

- Interest payable and bad debts are the special deduction issues.

- Bad-debt relief follows one of three approaches: commercial accounts, regulatory, or charge-off.

- Trading assets and financial instruments are usually marked to market, with accrual treatment for documented hedges.

- International issues dominate for multinational banks: double tax relief, branches vs subsidiaries, transfer pricing.

- Turnover taxes on gross income are not seen as best practice.

- Data reflects roughly 2002–2004 practice, so it is dated.

#### 4.1.1 What counts as "banking"

**Summary:** Traditional core functions were accepting deposits, honouring cheques, keeping current accounts, and lending, with income from the interest margin. Modern banks earn much from fees and trade instruments on their own account, and insurers and others now do bank-like business, so tax law rarely defines banking in statute. **Key highlights**

- Retail banks serve many small customers; wholesale banks (investment and merchant) serve few large ones.

- Tax treats as banking whatever regulators treat as banking.

#### 4.1.2 Income from assets: interest, discounts, dividends

**Summary:** Interest, discounts, and premiums are normally taxed on an accrual basis (when earned, not when received), with mark-to-market in some trading cases. Dividends between resident companies are normally not taxed again, except when the shares are held as trading stock. **Key highlights**

- Accrual: income recognized in the period earned.

- Dividends on trading-stock shares are taxed as income.

#### 4.1.3 Gains and losses on realizing assets

**Summary:** Gains and losses on fixed assets are recognized on realization. Assets held to meet payment demands or for trading are increasingly marked to market. Loan novations, assignments, swaps, and securitizations can create losses, which are recognized currently if done at arm's length. **Key highlights**

- Mark to market: value by what the asset could be exchanged for between informed, willing parties.

- Stock lending and repurchase agreements (REPOs) are taxed by their substance, not their legal form.

#### 4.1.4 Fees and other income

**Summary:** Fees from retail services, custody and advice, capital-market work, guarantees, and debt factoring are recognized when earned and receivable. Fees for multi-year facilities may be spread over time. **Key highlights**

- Same rule for commercial accounting and tax.

#### 4.1.5 Interest payable

**Summary:** Interest a bank pays is normally deductible on an accrual basis. Exceptions apply when the "interest" looks like a dividend: above-market rates, payments tied to profits, or limited-recourse loans. **Key highlights**

- Interest on core regulatory capital (Tier 1, 2, 3) is not deductible in principle.

- Banks have used offshore vehicles to create capital that is deductible; this links to thin capitalization.

- Disallowed interest may be taxed like a dividend.

#### 4.1.6 Bad debts and loan-loss provisions

**Summary:** Relief for impaired debts is especially important for banks. Specific provisions (for identified problem loans) are usually relieved, with clawback if the debt is repaid or the provision proves excessive; general provisions (against possible future problems) get less relief. **Key highlights**

- Commercial accounts approach: follows accounting rules, often specific provisions only.

- Regulatory approach: ties relief to the minimum provisions the regulator requires, often disallowing general provisions.

- Charge-off approach: relief only after debts are written off as irrecoverable; considered too restrictive.

- Unpaid interest on impaired loans goes to a suspense account and is taxed if later received.

- Recapitalization: if public funds cover a bank's losses, the matching tax losses are written off too (no "double dipping").

- Table 1.1 examples (c. 2002): general provisions deductible in China (1% domestic, 3% foreign-invested enterprises), Singapore, Japan, Germany, and Italy with limits; not deductible in the UK, Netherlands, Hong Kong, Australia, Korea, New Zealand, or the US.

#### 4.1.7 Derivatives and other financial instruments

**Summary:** Banks hold instruments for client service, trading, hedging, speculation, and as intermediaries. The usual practice is to mark them to market. The main exception is instruments held to hedge the bank's own risks, which may use accrual treatment if the hedge is documented and the other side is not marked to market. **Key highlights**

- Consistency principle: both sides of a hedge valued the same way.

- Detailed treatment is left to another chapter of the volume.

#### 4.1.8 Foreign exchange gains and losses

**Summary:** Companies generally keep accounts in domestic currency. Foreign exchange gains and losses on monetary items, most of a bank's assets and liabilities, are recognized at the balance-sheet date. Fixed and intangible assets are converted at purchase and recognized on disposal. **Key highlights**

- Hedged fixed assets may offset gains and losses so only the net is taxed.

- Rarely, banks working mainly in US dollars may keep tax accounts in foreign currency.

#### 4.1.9 Double tax relief

**Summary:** Banks generally get the same foreign tax credit as other businesses, limited to the tax that would apply domestically, with any excess deductible as a cost. Lending abroad creates a special problem because foreign withholding tax hits gross interest while the bank's profit is only the margin. **Key highlights**

- Some countries cap credit relief on interest (e.g., 15%).

- Anti-avoidance rules target routing through connected businesses.

- "Tax sparing" recognizes foreign tax that was spared by the foreign country's incentives.

#### 4.1.10 Foreign banks, branches, and transfer pricing

**Summary:** Double tax agreements and OECD guidelines govern branches and subsidiaries. Interest a branch pays its foreign head office is not normally deductible, except on loans made in ordinary banking business. Transfer pricing aims to ensure connected-party transactions reflect arm's-length prices. **Key highlights**

- OECD splits lending into six components; the first four (sourcing, assessing, negotiating, deciding) matter most in assigning profit.

- Investment banking profits are hard to locate, so a profit-splitting formula may be used.

- Modern finance moves information and assets across borders at the click of a button, which makes enforcement harder.

#### 4.1.11 Turnover taxes on financial institutions

**Summary:** Some countries tax the gross interest and other income of banks. These raise revenue without needing to compute net profit, but ignore ability to pay and hit thin-margin businesses hard. **Key highlights**

- Not seen as best practice and absent from most modern tax systems.
